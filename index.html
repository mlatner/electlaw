Project Overview

electlaw

A statutory and administrative implementation guide to proportional representation for U.S. lawyers and administrators.

What this project is

electlaw is a practitioner-oriented analytical document and user's guide for U.S. lawyers and administrators on the statutory and administrative implementation of proportional representation — at the federal, state, and local level, in both partisan and non-partisan contexts.

It is conceived as a much larger and more detailed follow-up to the Charles Hamilton Houston Institute / Guinier Project primer Getting from Votes to Seats: A Primer on Electoral System Design. Where the primer answers what the components of an electoral system are, this project answers how to codify and administer them in the United States.

The deliverable is a legal-document reference. Case studies must rely on actual statutory text and codes, not on journalism or summaries. Every recommendation is grounded in a working foreign provision and an explicit assessment of its portability to U.S. constitutional and administrative environments.

The three primary components — the core legal structure

This is a guide to writing PR election law into existence and getting it legislatively adopted. For each primary component, the user's-guide question is not what it is — the primer answers that — but how it is constructed in law: which legal instrument carries the choice, what statutory language has worked in working systems, and what political-adoption considerations follow. The case profiles distill drafting models for each component, with explicit U.S.-portability assessments.

1. Seat Product — how assembly size and district magnitude are codified

The first drafting question is where assembly size and district magnitude live and how amendable they are. Working systems split across three patterns:

  • Population-tiered table in a municipal code. Bavaria's Gemeindeordnung Art. 31 sets council size by a fixed table — 8 members up to 1,000 residents, 12 up to 2,000, and so on through to 80 for Munich. The table forecloses the political fight over council size that dominates U.S. local-reform debates and ties expansion to demographic fact rather than ad-hoc bargaining.
  • Fixed count in primary legislation. The federal Bundeswahlgesetz § 1(1): "Der Deutsche Bundestag besteht aus 630 Abgeordneten" — the Bundestag consists of 630 members. A single-sentence statutory cap.
  • Council-by-council choice within state law. NSW councils choose between 5, 7, 9, 12, or 15 members and between ward-divided or undivided structure under the Local Government Act 1993; the choice is set in each council's constitution.

For U.S. drafters, the threshold question is which legal instrument carries the fix — charter, state statute, or constitution. Charter amendment is the usual local lever; state-constitutional clauses may foreclose certain choices and should be checked first. The Threshold of Exclusion = 1 / (M + 1) belongs in the legislative findings so the political case for the chosen district magnitude sits on the record.

Districting authority and criteria. Once magnitude is set, districts must be drawn. The drafting questions are who has authority to draw the lines, what criteria govern, and how often the lines are reviewed. The federal Bundeswahlgesetz § 3 entrusts a permanent Wahlkreiskommission — appointed by the Federal President and including a Federal Statistical Office representative and a Federal Administrative Court judge — with periodic review and proposed redistricting; statutory criteria require respect for Länder boundaries, population deviation under 15% (tightening to 10% from 1 January 2026), coherence of area, and respect for municipal boundaries. Bavarian and NRW local councils sit within a single municipal district by default, sidestepping intra-municipal districting altogether. NSW councils choose between ward-divided and undivided structures.

For U.S. PR adoption, districting law is the densest body of existing constitutional doctrine the new statute will sit alongside: Reynolds v. Sims on one-person-one-vote, Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act, and state-constitutional districting clauses. PR with varying magnitudes (Switzerland, the LA proposal in the Guinier primer at p. 8) introduces wrinkles purely single-seat redistricting law has not addressed. The drafter's task is to specify districting authority and criteria in the same statute as the magnitude choice, so the two are reviewed together.

2. Ballot Structure — how the law defines the voter's act

The drafting question is what the voter's mark means. Three working statutory models — each a single-sentence drafting keystone:

  • One vote, dual duty (NRW Kommunalwahlgesetz § 25): "Der Wähler hat eine Stimme" — the voter has one vote. The mark elects a constituency candidate and counts for that candidate's reserve list, which fills proportional balance seats. Operationally simple. Closed list within the reserve.
  • As many votes as seats, with cumulation and panachage (Bavaria GLKrWG Art. 34): voters give up to three votes to a single candidate (kumulieren) and split votes across lists (panaschieren). Maximum voter agency, at the cost of ballot complexity and longer counts.
  • Above-the-line group voting (NSW Local Government Act 1993 s 308C): the ballot has two sections. A single mark above the line is a vote for the group, with preferences flowing within that group only. Numbered preferences below the line override the group order. The single-mark option is the dominant voting mode in practice.

Each model also defines what list labels may appear and how non-party participants are accommodated. Bavaria's residual definition is one single-sentence statutory answer to the labeling problem (GLKrWG Art. 24(1)): Wählergruppen are "all other associations or groups of natural persons whose purpose is to participate in municipal or county elections" — with no required organizational form.

The U.S. drafter's choice among these models is a trade-off between voter agency and ballot simplicity, with measurable consequences for invalid-vote rates, count duration, and political acceptability. The 2024 NSW local elections produced invalid-vote rates under 1% on the single-mark option; comparable Bavarian open-list ballots run higher.

3. Allocation Formula — named or written procedurally

The first drafting question for the formula is whether to identify it by name (Sainte-Laguë, D'Hondt) or to write the procedure directly into the statute. Working systems have settled on procedural drafting:

"die Gesamtstimmenzahlen … nacheinander so lange durch 1, 3, 5, 7, 9 und so weiter geteilt" (Bavaria GLKrWG Art. 35(2)) — the total vote counts are divided in turn by 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, and so on. The eponym "Sainte-Laguë" appears nowhere in the statute.

The federal Bundeswahlgesetz § 5 and the NRW Kommunalwahlgesetz § 33 follow the same pattern: divisor adjusted iteratively to fit the available seats; standard arithmetic rounding; ties resolved by lot. The procedural style is portable to U.S. adoption, where familiarity with European eponyms cannot be assumed and the calculation must be reproducible from the statutory text alone.

Threshold drafting. A legal threshold (e.g., the German federal 5%) layers on top of the natural threshold of exclusion that district magnitude alone produces. Pure-percentage thresholds are subject to constitutional review: in BVerfG, 30 July 2024, 2 BvF 1/23, the German Federal Constitutional Court held that the 5% threshold without the Grundmandatsklausel (admission for a sub-threshold party that wins three or more constituency seats) is unconstitutional. A U.S. PR statute can anticipate analogous review by including an explicit backdoor — either a Grundmandatsklausel-style constituency-win path or an apparentement provision letting small lists pool for allocation.

Apparentement and intra-list assignment. Two further formula-adjacent drafting choices: apparentement (Denmark listeforbund; Finland vaaliliitto) lets small lists pool for allocation while keeping separate ballot identities. Intra-list seat assignment ranges from list-order (NRW closed reserve list) to pure preference voting (Bavaria GLKrWG Art. 36(1): "in der Reihenfolge ihrer Stimmenzahlen" — in the order of their vote totals). Tie-breaking is reliably handled by drawing of lots by the returning officer.

Connected areas of law

The mechanics of PR are embedded in a wider statutory and administrative environment. The guide treats these connected areas at the depth needed to explain how each one bears on the three primary components:

  • Voter eligibility and registration — defines the denominator for any threshold calculation.
  • Candidate, party, and list registration — the most heavily developed connected area; covers both standard partisan ballot access and the novel non-partisan list registration regime needed for many U.S. local charters.
  • Campaign finance and disclosure — especially the asymmetries between party and non-party participants.
  • Election management bodies — institutional design, independence, funding stability.
  • Vote count, certification, recounts — the point at which the allocation formula meets administrative practice.
  • Election dispute resolution — pre-election and post-election forums.
  • Media access — broadcast equal-time and digital-platform regulation as it bears on list visibility.

Comparative cases

Thirteen working systems provide the empirical bedrock. Each is documented using a consistent template that walks through the three primary components, the connected areas, and a portability assessment for U.S. implementation.

Germany — Federal (Bundestag)
Canonical MMP. Two-vote ballot. Sainte-Laguë. Post-2024 BVerfG threshold ruling. Done
Germany — Bavaria
Open-list with cumulation and panachage. Wählergruppen as residual non-party lists. Sainte-Laguë since 2018. Done
Germany — NRW
One-vote MMP-style closed list. Active constitutional jurisprudence on threshold and formula choice. Done
Australia — NSW Local
Optional preferential PR with above-the-line group voting — the canonical one-mark-or-detailed-preferences ballot architecture. Done
Brazil
Party-list system with single vote that may be cast either for a candidate (voto nominal) or for the party (voto de legenda). The largest-scale operation of the candidate-or-party voting interface in the project's case set. Post-2017 abolition of coligações; 2024 STF rulings on performance thresholds. Done
Spain
Agrupación de electores under LOREG — the closed-list non-party list model. Planned
Netherlands
Open list with single candidate vote and a calibrated voorkeursdrempel (25%/50% of kiesdeler) for intra-list reordering. Lokale partijen as a universal-party-framework local list ecosystem. Magnitude-conditional remainder method (D'Hondt vs. largest remainders at M=19 boundary). Done
Finland
Pure open list; valitsijayhdistys as voter associations. Planned
Denmark
List-cartel mechanics (listeforbund); flexible vs. parallel-ordered list choice. Planned
Belgium
Three regional regimes; flexible list; Imperiali formula at municipal level. Planned
Chile
Post-2015 reform; independent-list mechanics; Constitutional Convention precedent. Planned
South Africa
Local mixed (ward + PR); independents on PR list since 2023. Planned
New Zealand
Canonical MMP at national level; reserved Māori seats; 5%-or-three-electorates threshold. Planned
Scotland
STV at local level — included for the administrative scaffolding lessons (candidate descriptions in lieu of party labels). Planned
United Kingdom
London Assembly only. Mostly a negative case. Planned

Diagnostic and reference tools

Four frameworks are applied to each provision under study. They are diagnostic lenses, not the project's core legal structure.

  • PROSeS (James 2020) — performance evaluation across five clusters: Process Design, Resource Investment, Service Output Quality, Service Outcomes, Stakeholder Satisfaction.
  • PEI eleven-stage electoral cycle (Norris / Electoral Integrity Project) — checklist of which connected-area provisions exist in a given case.
  • Venice Commission Code of Good Practice in Electoral Matters (2002) — a normative floor: universal, equal, free, secret, direct suffrage; periodicity; respect for fundamental rights; regulatory stability; procedural safeguards.
  • Gardner (ed.) Comparative Election Law (Edward Elgar 2022) — the universal-vs-particular portability test, applied to discipline the move from comparative description to U.S. recommendation.

Cross-cutting commitment

The project is committed to making equality in voting rights and minority representation an explicit dimension of comparison — alongside the three primary components — when synthesizing across cases. This is consistent with the project's lineage in the Guinier Project / Charles Hamilton Houston Institute and its audience of U.S. reformers working on minority representation.
