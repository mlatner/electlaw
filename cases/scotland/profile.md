# Scotland — Scottish Parliament (Holyrood) and Local Government

## 0. Case metadata

- **Country**: United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland —
  Scotland (a constituent nation with a devolved legislature).
- **Sub-jurisdiction**: All of Scotland. Two distinct electoral
  regimes are profiled here:
  - **Scottish Parliament** (Holyrood), 129 members. **Additional
    Member System (AMS)** — the UK statutory name for what is
    elsewhere termed mixed-member proportional (MMP).
  - **Local government**, 32 unitary councils. **Single
    Transferable Vote (STV)** in multi-member wards since 2007.
- **Levels of government covered**: regional (devolved national
  legislature) and local. UK Westminster elections in Scottish
  constituencies use FPTP and are out of scope for this profile.
- **Electoral system families**:
  - Holyrood: **MMP / AMS** — 73 constituency seats elected by
    plurality (FPTP) plus 56 regional list seats elected by
    closed list under **Jefferson/D'Hondt** with a modified
    divisor for constituency-correction.
  - Local government: **STV** treated here as administrative
    scaffolding, not as the analytical centre.
- **Governing statutes**:
  - **Scotland Act 1998** (1998 c. 46), UK Parliament — the
    foundational devolution statute creating the Scottish
    Parliament and prescribing its electoral system.
    <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1998/46/contents>
  - **Scottish Parliament (Elections etc.) Order 2015** (S.I.
    2015/425), as amended — the principal implementing order
    for Holyrood elections, replacing the 2010 Order.
    <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2015/425/contents/made>
  - **Local Governance (Scotland) Act 2004** (2004 asp 9),
    Scottish Parliament — introduced STV for local council
    elections from 2007.
    <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/asp/2004/9/contents>
  - **Scottish Local Government Elections Order 2007** (S.S.I.
    2007/42), reissued and amended (current consolidated form:
    Scottish Local Government Elections Order 2011 S.S.I.
    2011/399, with subsequent amendments).
    <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ssi/2011/399/contents/made>
  - **Representation of the People Act 1983** (1983 c. 2) — UK
    Parliament. Continues to apply in Scotland for matters not
    displaced by devolved statutes (e.g., parts of the
    franchise, electoral offences).
    <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1983/2/contents>
  - **Scottish Elections (Reform) Act 2020** (2020 asp 12) —
    lowered the franchise to 16, extended the franchise to
    foreign nationals with leave to remain, and altered other
    procedural matters.
    <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/asp/2020/12/contents>
  - **Scottish Elections (Franchise and Representation) Act
    2020** (2020 asp 6) — companion franchise extension act.
    <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/asp/2020/6/contents>
  - **Sex Discrimination (Election Candidates) Act 2002**
    (2002 c. 2) and **Equality Act 2010**, ss. 104–105 — UK
    Parliament; permit (but do not require) registered parties
    to operate single-sex shortlists.
    <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2002/2/contents>
    <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/15/section/104>
- **Constitutional anchor**: the UK has **no codified
  constitution**. The Scotland Act 1998 is itself a UK
  Parliament statute and functions as the de facto
  constitutional instrument for Scottish devolution. Section
  28(7) of the Scotland Act 1998 expressly preserves the
  authority of the UK Parliament to legislate for Scotland; the
  **Sewel Convention** (statutorily recognised in s. 2 of the
  Scotland Act 2016 inserting s. 28(8) into the 1998 Act) provides
  that the UK Parliament will "not normally" legislate on
  devolved matters without the consent of the Scottish
  Parliament.
- **Boundary authorities**:
  - **Boundary Commission for Scotland** — UK statutory body
    under the Parliamentary Constituencies Act 1986; reviews
    Scottish Parliament constituencies under the Scotland Act
    1998 Schedule 1.
  - **Local Government Boundary Commission for Scotland** —
    Scottish statutory body under the Local Government
    (Boundaries) (Scotland) Act 1973 (1973 c. 65) and the Local
    Government etc. (Scotland) Act 1994; reviews council areas
    and wards.
- **Last regular elections**:
  - Holyrood — **6 May 2021**. Next regular: 7 May 2026.
  - Local government — **5 May 2022**. Next regular: 7 May 2027
    (four-year cycle as set by the 2004 Act).
- **Analyst**: this profile, 2026-05-15.

### Why this case is in the project

Three features make Scotland a documented reference for the
project:

1. **Two co-located but statutorily separate PR regimes operate
   in the same territory under different formulas.** Holyrood
   uses closed-list AMS/MMP with Jefferson/D'Hondt allocation
   across eight regional tiers; local councils use STV in
   multi-member wards under a separate Act of the Scottish
   Parliament. The two regimes share an electorate, an EMB
   (the Electoral Management Board for Scotland from 2011),
   and a constitutional frame, but use entirely different
   ballot architectures and conversion rules. This produces
   the project's clearest example of **regime layering inside
   a single sub-national jurisdiction**.

2. **The Scotland Act 1998 illustrates how a non-codified
   constitutional system anchors a PR regime through ordinary
   statute.** Sections 1–6 and Schedules 1–2 of the 1998 Act
   specify assembly size, district structure, and allocation
   formula at the level a written constitution would in
   Germany or Spain. For US drafters working in jurisdictions
   where state constitutions are difficult to amend, the
   Scottish architecture demonstrates a path by which a PR
   regime can be codified primarily in statute, with a
   higher-order instrument (here, a UK Act with quasi-
   constitutional status) setting only the framework.

3. **The local-government STV regime is the project's
   reference case for STV-as-administrative-scaffolding.**
   Per the project's standing rule, STV is treated as a
   ballot-architecture and counting protocol rather than as a
   list-PR vote-conversion mechanism. Scotland's STV regime
   provides documented statutory text on candidate
   "descriptions" (the registered label that appears on the
   ballot in lieu of, or alongside, a party label), ballot
   ordering, and the surplus-transfer count administered by
   returning officers under the 2007/2011 Order. The
   substantive analytical weight in this profile sits on the
   Holyrood AMS section.

The Holyrood section is the analytical centre. The local-
government STV section documents the statutory architecture
that produces multi-member wards and grouped candidacies, but
does not centre vote-conversion mechanics.

## 1. Statutory architecture — devolution and the two regimes

The Scottish electoral landscape is governed by three layers of
statute:

| Layer | Instrument | Subject |
|---|---|---|
| UK Parliament (foundational) | **Scotland Act 1998** | Creates the Scottish Parliament; specifies AMS, assembly size, regions, and constituencies (ss. 1–6, Schedules 1–2). |
| Scottish Parliament (devolved) | **Local Governance (Scotland) Act 2004**; **Scottish Elections (Reform) Act 2020**; **Scottish Elections (Franchise and Representation) Act 2020** | Local-government STV; franchise extension; procedural reform. |
| Secondary legislation | **Scottish Parliament (Elections etc.) Order 2015**; **Scottish Local Government Elections Order 2011** | Implementing detail — nomination, ballot, count, return. |

The **Scotland Act 2016** (UK Parliament) devolved further
electoral competence to the Scottish Parliament, including
power to legislate for Holyrood elections themselves (s. 3,
inserting new s. 12 into the Scotland Act 1998). After 2016, the
Scottish Parliament can amend the Holyrood franchise and
candidate eligibility, but the **core electoral system —
assembly size, AMS structure, regions, allocation formula — is
"protected" and may be modified only by a two-thirds super-
majority of the Scottish Parliament** (Scotland Act 1998 s.
31(1A), inserted by Scotland Act 2016 s. 11). This is the most
analytically important provision in the architecture for
regulatory-stability purposes (Venice Commission point).

### Section 31(1A) text — protected provisions

> "A Bill which makes provision about—
>   (a) the persons entitled to vote at an election for
>       membership of the Parliament,
>   (b) the system by which members of the Parliament are
>       returned,
>   (c) the number of constituencies, regions or any
>       equivalent electoral area,
>   (d) the number of members to be returned for each
>       constituency, region or equivalent electoral area,
> shall not be passed unless the number of members voting in
> favour of it at the final stage is at least two-thirds of the
> total number of seats for members of the Parliament."

(Scotland Act 1998, s. 31(4), as inserted by Scotland Act 2016
s. 11.)

For US drafters: a statutory super-majority requirement on the
core electoral system, inside an otherwise ordinary-statute
regime, is a directly portable drafting device. It places
the electoral mechanics under a higher procedural barrier than
ordinary legislation without requiring constitutional
amendment.

### Statute-instrument cascade

Operational interpretation of the regimes runs through:

1. **Scotland Act 1998** (UK Parliament) — sets the framework
   for Holyrood.
2. **Scottish Parliament (Elections etc.) Order 2015** —
   implementing Order made under s. 12 of the Scotland Act
   1998. Covers nomination procedure, ballot paper format,
   counting procedure, declaration of result.
3. **Returning Officer** guidance — issued by the Electoral
   Management Board for Scotland (EMB) under the Local
   Electoral Administration (Scotland) Act 2011.
4. **Electoral Commission** guidance — issued by the UK
   Electoral Commission under the Political Parties, Elections
   and Referendums Act 2000 (PPERA).
5. **Judicial review** — through the Court of Session and the
   UK Supreme Court.

The Electoral Management Board for Scotland (EMB), established
by the **Local Electoral Administration (Scotland) Act 2011**
(2011 asp 2), coordinates local-government administration
across the 32 council areas; its Convener has statutory
direction-giving authority. This is a recent (post-2011)
coordinating layer; before 2011, local administration was
entirely council-by-council.

## 2. Component 1 — Seat Product (Assembly Size × District Magnitude)

### Scottish Parliament (Holyrood)

#### Statutory text — Scotland Act 1998, s. 1(2)–(3)

> (2) The Parliament shall consist of—
>   (a) one member for each constituency (referred to in this
>       Act as "a constituency member"), and
>   (b) members for each region (referred to in this Act as
>       "regional members").
>
> (3) Members of the Parliament shall be returned in accordance
>     with the provisions of this Act about the elections and
>     membership of the Parliament.

#### Statutory text — Scotland Act 1998, s. 1(5) and Schedule 1

Section 1(5) provides that the constituencies and regions are
those set out in Schedule 1, as amended from time to time by
order under s. 12. Schedule 1 paragraphs 1–4 set:

- **Total seats**: **129** (s. 1(2) read with Schedule 1).
- **73 constituency seats**, each elected by a single member
  under FPTP (s. 1(4)(a) and Schedule 1 para. 1).
- **8 regions**, each returning **7 regional members** (s.
  1(4)(b) and Schedule 1 para. 2), for **56 regional seats**.
- Regions: Central Scotland, Glasgow, Highlands and Islands,
  Lothian, Mid Scotland and Fife, North East Scotland, South
  Scotland, West Scotland (Schedule 1 para. 2 list).

#### Constituency-region mapping

Constituencies are nested inside regions: each region contains
between 8 and 10 constituencies. The eight regions partition
the territory exhaustively. A voter casts (a) one constituency
vote in their constituency, and (b) one regional vote in their
region — separately specified on a single ballot paper.

#### Notes — Holyrood seat product

The 73:56 split (constituency:regional) is roughly 57:43. By
international comparison this is a slightly higher
constituency share than New Zealand's MMP (currently 72
electorate seats out of 120 total = 60%, with 48 list seats),
but lower than the German federal system pre-2023 reform
(299:299 = 50%). The constituency-share ratio is one of the
two parameters (the other being the threshold) that
determines how much proportional correction the list tier
delivers; a 43% list share, distributed across 8 regional
sub-pools of 7 seats each, produces less aggregate
proportionality than a single national list tier of equivalent
size would.

The 8-region structure has a structural threshold of exclusion
of approximately 12.5% per region (1/(M+1) with M=7). A party
that does not clear that threshold in any single region wins no
regional seat from that region; its votes do not aggregate
across regions. This is the central design parameter
distinguishing Scotland's AMS from a national-list MMP design
(such as Germany or New Zealand), where the list tier operates
at national level and the structural threshold is much lower.

For US drafters: the Scottish Holyrood architecture
demonstrates that **MMP can be designed with a sub-national
list tier rather than a national list tier**. The trade-off is
documented: regional list tiers preserve geographic
representation in the list seats but reduce overall
proportionality and raise the effective threshold for small
parties. A US state legislature adopting MMP with sub-state
list regions (e.g., one per congressional district) would
produce similar effects.

### Local government (32 councils)

#### Statutory text — Local Governance (Scotland) Act 2004, s. 1

> (1) In place of the existing arrangements for the election
>     of councillors, councillors are to be elected by the
>     single transferable vote system.
>
> (2) An election under that system is to be held in
>     accordance with rules made under section 16 of the
>     Local Government (Scotland) Act 1973 (rules as to local
>     elections).

#### Statutory text — Local Governance (Scotland) Act 2004, s. 1(3)–(4) (ward structure)

> (3) For the purposes of the election of councillors—
>   (a) each local government area is to be divided into
>       wards, and
>   (b) at each election, each ward is to return three or
>       four councillors.
>
> (4) The Boundary Commission [for Scotland] is to make
>     recommendations as to the wards into which each local
>     government area is to be divided and the number of
>     councillors to be returned for each ward.

Section 1(3)(b) establishes a **statutory magnitude band: M =
3 or M = 4** for every ward in every council. There are no
single-member or two-member wards under the 2004 Act, and no
ward returns more than four councillors.

#### The 2018 amendment — Islands (Scotland) Act 2018

The **Islands (Scotland) Act 2018** (2018 asp 12), s. 8,
amended s. 1(3)(b) of the 2004 Act to permit **M = 1 or M = 2
in inhabited islands** within the three island councils
(Orkney, Shetland, Comhairle nan Eilean Siar). The provision
recognises that small island populations cannot be aggregated
into 3- or 4-member wards without crossing geographic divides.
This is the only statutory exception to the M = 3/4 band.

#### Council size and ward count

Council size is set by the **Local Government Boundary
Commission for Scotland** through periodic reviews under the
Local Government (Scotland) Act 1973 as amended. Council sizes
range from 18 councillors (Comhairle nan Eilean Siar) to 85
(Glasgow), determined by population. The number of wards per
council follows from council size and the M = 3/4 band; a
council of 60 councillors typically has 17–20 wards.

#### Notes — local government seat product

The statutory M = 3/4 band is a deliberate drafting choice. The
2004 Act could have permitted larger wards (M = 5 or M = 6
would produce lower structural thresholds and greater
proportionality) or smaller (M = 2 would produce something
closer to limited voting). The choice of 3/4 was made on the
recommendation of the **Kerley Committee** (Renewing Local
Democracy Working Group, 2000), which judged that M = 3/4 in
STV wards balanced geographic accountability with
proportional outcomes for the major parties present in
Scottish council areas. The structural threshold of 20–25% at
ward level is therefore substantially higher than the Holyrood
regional threshold (12.5%) and very substantially higher than
the effective threshold in larger-magnitude list-PR systems
(e.g., Netherlands gemeenteraad M = 9–45, threshold 2.2–10%).

For US drafters: the 2004 Act offers a documented model of a
statute that **explicitly bands magnitude** rather than
fixing a single magnitude or leaving it to a boundary
commission. The single-sentence rule "each ward is to return
three or four councillors" (s. 1(3)(b)) is portable. The 2018
island-exception amendment shows how a boundary commission can
be given statutory permission to depart from the band in
specifically defined circumstances.

## 3. Component 2 — Ballot Structure

### Holyrood — two-vote AMS ballot

#### Statutory text — Scotland Act 1998, s. 6 (regional vote)

> (1) At a general election, each person entitled to vote as
>     elector at the election in a constituency included in a
>     region may give a regional vote (which may be a vote for
>     a registered political party or for an individual
>     candidate to be a regional member).
>
> (2) Where—
>   (a) a vote is given for a registered political party which
>       has submitted a list of candidates to be regional
>       members for the region, and
>   (b) the vote is duly given,
> the vote shall be treated as a vote for the list of
> candidates submitted by that party in the region.

Section 5 establishes the parallel **constituency vote** (FPTP
in single-member constituencies). The two votes are cast on a
single ballot paper that displays both contests side by side;
the voter marks one preference in each column.

#### The regional list

Per Scotland Act 1998, s. 5A (inserted by the Scottish
Parliament (Constituencies) Act 2004 and subsequent
consolidating amendments), and the Scottish Parliament
(Elections etc.) Order 2015, **regional lists are closed**:

- A registered political party submits an **ordered list** of
  up to **12 candidates** for each region in which it stands.
- The submitted order is binding. The voter cannot reorder, and
  there is no preference vote on the list.
- Independent candidates may stand as **individual regional
  candidates** under s. 6(1); they appear on the ballot in
  their own right without a party label.

This is the closed-list-with-individual-candidate-option
architecture also seen in Germany (BWahlG § 30) and New
Zealand (Electoral Act 1993 s. 127).

#### Local government — STV ballot

#### Statutory text — Local Governance (Scotland) Act 2004, s. 4 (ballot paper)

> (1) The ballot paper for a local government election is to
>     be in such form as may be prescribed.
>
> (2) The form prescribed must enable an elector to express a
>     preference for as many or as few candidates as the
>     elector wishes by placing the numerals 1, 2, 3 and so on
>     against the names of those candidates.

The Scottish Local Government Elections Order 2011 (S.S.I.
2011/399), Schedule 1 paragraph 21, sets the ballot-paper
form. Candidates appear in **alphabetical order by surname**;
each candidate's entry shows the candidate's name, address,
and **registered description** (see Section B below).

A voter may rank as many or as few candidates as the voter
wishes, in any order, using the numerals 1, 2, 3, etc. There is
no statutory requirement to rank all candidates; partial
ranking is valid.

#### Candidate "descriptions" — the project's key feature

For the project's purposes, the most analytically relevant
local-government provision is the **statutory mechanism for
candidate descriptions** that appear on the ballot in lieu of,
or alongside, a party name. This is governed by:

- **Political Parties, Elections and Referendums Act 2000**
  (PPERA), Schedule 4 — registration of party names and
  descriptions with the Electoral Commission.
- **Scottish Local Government Elections Order 2011** Schedule
  1 paras. 8–9 — what may appear on the ballot.

A candidate may appear with:
1. A **registered party name** (if the candidate is a party
   candidate authorised by the party's nominating officer);
2. A **registered party description** (a phrase up to six words
   that the party has registered with the Electoral
   Commission, e.g., "Scottish National Party candidate for
   Glasgow North");
3. The label "**Independent**" (a statutorily reserved
   description for non-party candidates);
4. **No description** (the candidate appears with name and
   address only).

A non-party group of candidates standing together cannot
register a joint "description" outside the PPERA party-
registration framework; each individual non-party candidate
appears as "Independent" without a group identifier.

For the project: this is the documented limit of the Scottish
administrative scaffolding for non-party groupings. **There is
no statutory mechanism in Scotland equivalent to the Bavarian
*Wählergruppe* registration or the Dutch *lokale partij* local-
only party registration.** A non-party group that wishes to
appear as a unified slate on the Scottish local ballot must
register as a political party with the Electoral Commission
under PPERA — incurring the registration fee, the obligation
to file accounts, and the regulatory exposure that party
registration entails.

#### Notes — ballot architecture

The Holyrood ballot is a **two-vote ballot on a single sheet**.
Voters mark a constituency preference in one column and a
regional preference in the other column. The two votes are
counted separately. Voters may "split" their votes (vote for
one party at constituency level and a different party at
regional level), and a substantial fraction do so at each
election. This is the standard MMP ballot design; New Zealand
(Electoral Act 1993 s. 150), Germany (BWahlG § 30), and pre-
2002 Bavarian *Landtagswahlen* all use structurally similar
two-vote ballot architectures.

The local-government STV ballot is a single-column ranked
ballot with candidates listed alphabetically. The 2011 Order
does **not** provide for ballot rotation (Robson rotation, as
used in the Australian Capital Territory and Tasmania) or any
other randomisation of candidate order. The alphabetical
listing produces a documented "**donkey vote**" effect — a
modest but measurable advantage for candidates whose surnames
appear earlier in the alphabet — which has been the subject of
academic and Scottish Parliament committee analysis. The
Kerley Committee considered Robson rotation in 2000 and
recommended against it on grounds of administrative
complexity; the 2004 Act adopted alphabetical-order listing
without subsequent revision.

For US drafters: the Scottish administrative scaffolding for
candidate descriptions (PPERA Schedule 4 + 2011 Order Schedule
1) is the project's reference for what a **non-party-label
regime looks like when there is no separate non-party-group
entity class**. The result is that grouped non-party
candidacies are not statutorily supported; the only options
are individual independents (with "Independent" description)
or registration as a political party. For US local-elections
contexts where many candidacies are explicitly non-partisan,
the Scottish approach is a documented negative reference: it
demonstrates what happens when the statutory framework forces
non-party candidates either into individual posture or into the
party-registration machinery.

### Statutory candidate-diversity provisions

#### Sex Discrimination (Election Candidates) Act 2002 / Equality Act 2010, ss. 104–105

Section 104 of the Equality Act 2010 (re-enacting and
broadening the 2002 Act) provides that registered political
parties **may** lawfully reserve places on candidate
shortlists, or whole shortlists, for women — notwithstanding
the general sex-discrimination provisions of the Act. The
provision is permissive: it allows parties to operate all-
women shortlists without breaching the Equality Act, but does
not require any party to do so.

> Equality Act 2010 s. 104:
> (1) This section applies to an association which is a
>     registered political party.
> (2) A person does not contravene this Part only by acting in
>     accordance with selection arrangements.
> (3) "Selection arrangements" are arrangements—
>   (a) which the party makes for regulating the selection of
>       its candidates in a relevant election,
>   (b) the purpose of which is to reduce inequality in the
>       party's representation in the body concerned, and
>   (c) which, subject to subsection (7), are a proportionate
>       means of achieving that purpose.

The provision is set to expire ("sunset clause") in 2030
unless renewed.

In practice: **Scottish Labour** has used all-women shortlists
for Holyrood selection contests in selected constituencies and
list places; **the Scottish National Party (SNP)** has used a
"zipped" gender alternation rule in regional lists since 2002
(women and men alternating positions). Neither practice is
statutorily required; both are internal party rules permitted
by s. 104. The same s. 104 framework applies to local-
government candidate selection.

There is no statutory candidate-quota provision in either the
Scotland Act 1998 or the Local Governance (Scotland) Act 2004
or their implementing orders. The diversity architecture is
permissive (Equality Act s. 104 permits party-internal
shortlist rules) rather than mandatory (no Spanish-style 40%
balanced-composition requirement applies).

For US drafters: the UK approach to candidate diversity is the
**permissive** model — statute allows parties to operate
selection-restrictive practices without breaching anti-
discrimination law, but does not impose any list-composition
requirement. This contrasts with the Spanish **mandatory** model
(LOREG Art. 44 bis), the Belgian gender-parity rule, and the
French parité law. The portability question for the US is
whether a state-statute analogue of Equality Act s. 104 (a
state-law safe harbour for party diversity practices) would
itself face state or federal constitutional challenge.

## 4. Component 3 — Allocation Formula

### Holyrood — modified Jefferson/D'Hondt across regions

#### Statutory text — Scotland Act 1998, s. 8 (allocation of regional seats)

> (1) The first regional member seat shall be allocated to
>     the registered political party or individual candidate
>     with the highest regional figure.
>
> (2) The second and subsequent regional member seats shall
>     be allocated to the registered political party or
>     individual candidate with the highest regional figure,
>     calculated after the previous allocation.
>
> (3) An individual candidate already returned as a
>     constituency member shall be disregarded.
>
> (4) Where a registered political party has had a candidate
>     returned as a constituency member, the regional figure
>     for that party shall be the total number of regional
>     votes given for the party in all the constituencies
>     included in the region, divided by the aggregate of one
>     plus the number of candidates of the party returned as
>     constituency members for any of those constituencies.
>
> (5) Where a registered political party has not had a
>     candidate returned as a constituency member, the
>     regional figure for that party shall be the total number
>     of regional votes given for the party in all the
>     constituencies included in the region, divided by one.
>
> (6) Where a registered political party has had one or more
>     candidates returned as a regional member for the region,
>     the regional figure for that party shall be the total
>     number of regional votes given for the party in all the
>     constituencies included in the region, divided by the
>     aggregate of one, the number of candidates of the party
>     returned as constituency members for any of those
>     constituencies, and the number of candidates of the
>     party returned as a regional member for the region.

(Scotland Act 1998 s. 8, as in force.)

#### Mechanism — the modified divisor

Section 8 implements a **Jefferson/D'Hondt highest-averages
procedure**, with the modification that the divisor for each
party's regional figure starts at **(1 + constituency seats
already won)** rather than at 1. Algebraically:

- For each party with C constituency seats won in the region:
  initial regional figure = (regional votes) / (1 + C)
- After each regional seat awarded to the party, divisor
  increments by 1.
- For each independent regional candidate: figure = regional
  votes (single divisor, since the candidate cannot win
  additional list seats).
- Seats awarded to highest regional figure, recomputed after
  each award.

This is **compensatory allocation**: parties that did well in
constituency seats have their regional figure correspondingly
deflated, producing approximate proportionality across the
combined seat allocation per region. The modification of the
initial divisor (from 1 to 1+C) is the distinctive AMS feature
relative to pure list D'Hondt.

#### Worked example — illustrative (not actual)

Consider a region with 7 list seats, where:
- Party A wins 5 constituency seats with 90,000 regional votes.
- Party B wins 1 constituency seat with 50,000 regional votes.
- Party C wins 0 constituency seats with 40,000 regional votes.
- Party D wins 0 constituency seats with 25,000 regional votes.

Initial regional figures:
- A: 90,000 / (1 + 5) = 15,000
- B: 50,000 / (1 + 1) = 25,000
- C: 40,000 / (1 + 0) = 40,000
- D: 25,000 / (1 + 0) = 25,000

Seat 1 to C (highest at 40,000). C's divisor now becomes 2:
40,000 / 2 = 20,000.

Seat 2 to B or D (both at 25,000); tie-break by Scotland Act
1998 s. 8(7) (lot drawn by returning officer). Suppose B
takes seat 2; B's divisor becomes 3: 50,000 / 3 = 16,667.

Seat 3 to D (at 25,000). D's divisor becomes 2: 12,500.

Seat 4 to C (at 20,000). C's divisor becomes 3: 13,333.

Seat 5 to B (at 16,667). B's divisor becomes 4: 12,500.

Seat 6 to A (at 15,000). A's divisor becomes 7: 12,857.

Seat 7 to A (at 12,857). A's divisor becomes 8.

Final allocation:
- A: 5 constituency + 2 regional = 7 seats
- B: 1 constituency + 2 regional = 3 seats
- C: 0 constituency + 2 regional = 2 seats
- D: 0 constituency + 1 regional = 1 seat

This is illustrative; actual 2021 results vary by region. The
worked example demonstrates that the compensatory mechanism
produces seat shares approximately proportional to regional
votes, with the constituency-tier results "absorbed" through
the divisor modification.

#### Tie-breaking — s. 8(7)

> (7) Where the calculation of regional figures under this
>     section produces a tie between two or more political
>     parties or candidates, the seat shall be awarded by the
>     drawing of lots.

Drawing of lots is conducted by the Regional Returning Officer.

### Local government — STV count

#### Statutory text — Local Governance (Scotland) Act 2004, s. 5

> (1) The Scottish Ministers may by order prescribe the rules
>     according to which the votes cast at a local government
>     election are to be counted.

Implementation: **Scottish Local Government Elections Order
2011** (S.S.I. 2011/399), Schedule 1, Parts III–VI, sets the
count rules. The count uses the **Weighted Inclusive Gregory
Method (WIGM)** with the **Droop quota**.

#### Droop quota — 2011 Order Schedule 1 para. 49

> The quota is determined by dividing the total number of
> valid votes by one more than the number of councillors to
> be returned, ignoring any remainder.

Algebraically: Quota = floor[ (total valid first-preferences /
(M + 1)) ] + 1.

For M = 4: a candidate needs > 20% of valid first-preferences
(or transfers up to that level) to be elected; for M = 3: > 25%.

#### Surplus transfer — WIGM

Under WIGM, a candidate whose vote total exceeds the quota has
the surplus transferred to subsequent preferences with a
**transfer value** computed as:

  transfer_value = (candidate's surplus) / (candidate's total)

Each ballot that contributed to that candidate's total is then
"forwarded" to its next-preference continuing candidate at the
transfer value. WIGM differs from the simpler "Inclusive
Gregory" (used in NSW) and from "Random Sample" (used in
Ireland local elections) in that the transfer value is
calculated to four decimal places and applied per ballot,
preserving precision in cases where multiple surplus
transfers occur.

#### Exclusion — 2011 Order Schedule 1 paras. 52–55

When no continuing candidate has the quota and seats remain to
be filled, the candidate with the lowest current vote total is
**excluded**; that candidate's votes (held at their current
transfer values) are redistributed to next preferences. The
process iterates until M candidates have been elected.

#### Notes — Holyrood allocation

The Holyrood AMS formula is the project's documented case for
**compensatory MMP allocation with sub-national tiers**. The
divisor modification at s. 8(4) is the operative mechanism;
without it, the regional list would be parallel rather than
compensatory.

Three statutory features warrant note:

1. **No statutory percentage threshold.** Unlike the German
   federal 5% threshold (BWahlG § 6(3)) or the New Zealand 5%
   / one-electorate threshold (Electoral Act 1993 s. 191), the
   Scottish regime imposes no minimum vote share. The effective
   threshold is the structural threshold of 12.5% per region,
   modulated by the constituency-correction divisor.

2. **Regional tier as ceiling, not as leveling.** The German
   and New Zealand MMP regimes use a national list tier (and,
   in Germany, mechanisms for overhang seats) so that the
   final seat share is proportional to the national vote
   share. The Scottish regime caps the list tier at 7 seats
   per region. A party that wins more constituency seats than
   its proportional share in a region simply keeps those
   seats; there is **no national-level leveling**. This
   produces the documented effect that a party with strong
   constituency results (e.g., the SNP) regularly holds a
   higher share of seats than its share of regional votes —
   the "overhang" effect — without compensating leveling
   seats.

3. **Procedural codification of the formula.** Scotland Act
   1998 s. 8 specifies the modified D'Hondt procedure directly
   in the statute, without using the eponym "D'Hondt" or
   "Jefferson." This continues the pattern documented across
   the project's case set: working statutes consistently
   choose procedural over named drafting for allocation
   formulas.

For US drafters: the Scottish design provides a documented
working example of MMP **without a statutory threshold** and
**without national leveling**. A US state-legislature MMP
design that omits both features will produce similar effects:
a regional list tier that softens but does not erase
disproportionality, with high-constituency-share parties
benefiting from de facto overhang.

#### Notes — local-government STV

The 2007 transition to STV is the most significant change to
Scottish local elections since 1973. Three observations from
the project's framework:

1. **STV at M = 3 or M = 4 with Droop quota produces
   structural thresholds of 20–25% per ward.** This is
   materially higher than the equivalent magnitudes under
   list-PR Hare-quota allocation. For dispersed minority
   communities concentrated in single wards, the threshold is
   achievable; for communities dispersed across multiple
   wards, the threshold operates as a constraint.

2. **The 2011 Order does not provide ballot rotation.**
   Alphabetical listing produces a measurable position
   advantage. For US drafters, the alphabetical-only
   convention is the documented default; jurisdictions
   adopting STV that wish to neutralise position effects must
   add a rotation provision explicitly (Tasmanian Hare-Clark
   model: Electoral Act 2004 (Tas) s. 96).

3. **The count is conducted electronically** under software
   certified by Scottish Ministers. The 2011 Order Schedule 1
   Part VI does not specify the software but requires
   certification; in practice, the EMB has standardised on a
   single vendor's package. This raises the project's
   recurrent "**counting-software certification**" question:
   how does the statute ensure that the rules in Schedule 1
   are accurately implemented in software, and what audit
   regime applies? The Scottish answer is statutory: the
   ministerial certification under s. 5(2) of the 2004 Act.

## 5. Connected areas

### A. Voter eligibility and registration

#### Holyrood franchise — post-2020 expansion

Per the **Scottish Elections (Franchise and Representation)
Act 2020** (2020 asp 6), the Holyrood franchise extends to:

- British and qualifying Commonwealth citizens 16+ resident
  in Scotland;
- EU and other foreign nationals 16+ with leave to remain in
  the UK and resident in Scotland;
- Prisoners serving sentences of less than 12 months (per the
  Scottish Elections (Reform) Act 2020 s. 5).

The 16-year-old franchise was first applied at the 2014
Scottish independence referendum (Scottish Independence
Referendum (Franchise) Act 2013) and extended permanently to
Holyrood and local elections by the **Scottish Elections
(Reduction of Voting Age) Act 2015** (2015 asp 7).

Voter registration is administered by the **Electoral
Registration Officer** for each council area, under the
Representation of the People Act 1983 and Regulations made
thereunder. Registration is by individual application; there
is no separate Scottish voter register (the UK-wide register
is used for all elections, with Scottish-specific franchise
attributes recorded).

#### Local-government franchise

The local-government franchise matches the Holyrood franchise
following the 2020 reforms. Pre-2020, local franchise was
narrower (UK and qualifying Commonwealth citizens, EU
citizens until Brexit transition).

### B. Candidate / list registration — *full subsection*

#### Holyrood — constituency candidates

Per Scottish Parliament (Elections etc.) Order 2015,
Schedule 2 (nomination):

- A candidate must be a **British, Irish, or qualifying
  Commonwealth citizen**, 18+, not disqualified by statute.
- **Nomination paper** signed by 1 proposer and 1 seconder
  (both registered electors in the constituency) plus 8
  assenters.
- **Deposit**: £500 per constituency candidate, refunded if
  the candidate wins at least 5% of valid votes (2015 Order
  Schedule 2 Part III).
- Party candidates require authorisation from the party's
  nominating officer (per PPERA s. 22).

#### Holyrood — regional list candidates

- A registered party submits an **ordered list of up to 12
  candidates** for each region.
- Individual regional candidates may stand without a party
  affiliation.
- **Deposit**: £500 per regional list (refunded if the list
  wins at least 5% of regional votes in the region).
- **List composition**: no statutory diversity quota;
  permissive party-internal rules under Equality Act 2010 s.
  104.

#### Local government — nomination

Per Scottish Local Government Elections Order 2011, Schedule
1, Part II:

- Each candidate is nominated **individually** (not as part of
  a list).
- **Nomination paper** signed by 1 proposer and 1 seconder
  (both registered electors in the ward) plus 8 assenters.
- **No deposit** is required for local government nominations
  (this is a documented difference from Holyrood and
  Westminster).
- Candidate registers their description with the Returning
  Officer (party name, party description, "Independent," or
  no description).

#### Notes — candidate registration architecture

The Scottish architecture distinguishes sharply between:
- **Party candidates** — authorised by a party's nominating
  officer under PPERA s. 22; may appear with party name or
  registered party description on the ballot;
- **Individual candidates** — nominated by ward/constituency
  electors; may appear as "Independent" or with no
  description.

There is no intermediate category. **The project's recurring
question — how to support non-party groupings administratively
while preserving non-party legal status — has no Scottish
answer.** A US drafter seeking models for non-party local-
slate administration must look to Bavaria (*Wählergruppen*),
the Netherlands (*lokale partijen*), or Spain (*agrupaciones
de electores*); the Scottish approach is the documented
opposite.

The **dual-candidacy** rule at Holyrood (a candidate may stand
both in a constituency and on the regional list within the
same region) is a portable drafting feature. New Zealand
(Electoral Act 1993 s. 154) and Germany (BWahlG § 4) permit
the same. Scotland's variant is statutorily identical: a
candidate who loses a constituency but is highly placed on the
regional list may still be elected through the list.

### C. Campaign finance

Campaign finance is governed jointly by:

- **Political Parties, Elections and Referendums Act 2000**
  (PPERA) — UK statute. Sets party registration, accounts,
  donation rules, and national campaign spending limits.
- **Representation of the People Act 1983** Part II — UK
  statute. Sets candidate spending limits and expense
  declaration.
- **Scottish Elections (Integrity and Reform) Act 2025** —
  Scottish Parliament Act. Adjusted candidate spending limits
  for Holyrood and local elections following the 2024
  Electoral Commission recommendations.

Candidate spending limits are stated in the relevant elections
order and updated periodically by ministerial regulation. The
Electoral Commission publishes annual party accounts; local
election expenses are submitted to the Returning Officer and
made publicly available.

There is **no statutory public funding of parties or
candidates** in the Scottish system. Parties are funded by
membership dues, donations, and (for parliamentary parties)
**Short money** at Westminster and the analogous Scottish
**Salisbury money** at Holyrood — both of which are
parliamentary-support funds for opposition parties' policy and
research work, not campaign funds.

### D. Election administration and EMB structure

The Scottish administrative architecture has three layers:

1. **Returning Officer** per constituency (Holyrood), region
   (Holyrood regional), or council (local). Statutory officer
   under the Representation of the People Act 1983 s. 24.
2. **Electoral Management Board for Scotland (EMB)** — created
   by the Local Electoral Administration (Scotland) Act 2011.
   Coordinates local-government administration across the 32
   council areas; the Convener of the EMB has statutory
   direction-giving authority over Returning Officers in
   local-government matters.
3. **Electoral Commission** — UK statutory body under PPERA.
   Regulates party registration, campaign finance, and
   broader integrity matters across the UK including
   Scotland.

The 2011 EMB statute is the documented architectural change
that produced coordinated Scottish local-election
administration. Pre-2011, each Returning Officer operated
independently under the same Order but without coordinating
direction. The EMB's establishment reduced the documented
variability in how the 2007 STV Order had been operationalised
across councils in the 2007 cycle.

For US drafters: the EMB is a portable model for **regional
electoral coordination** that sits between local Returning
Officers and a higher (state or federal) regulatory body. It is
particularly relevant for US state contexts where county
clerks administer elections under state law without
state-level coordinating direction; an "EMB-equivalent" would
add the coordinating layer without removing local-officer
statutory authority.

### E. Vote count, certification, dispute resolution

#### Count

- **Holyrood**: count conducted at constituency level
  immediately on close of poll. Regional results aggregated by
  the Regional Returning Officer. Manual counting of paper
  ballots; results typically declared overnight.
- **Local government**: **electronic STV count** at council
  level. Software certified by Scottish Ministers under
  Scottish Local Government Elections Order 2011 s. 5(2).
  Ballots scanned, count run by software, results published.
  The shift from manual to electronic counting in 2007
  reflected the practical infeasibility of manual STV counts
  at the scale of council elections.

#### Certification

Returning Officer's declaration of result is the formal
certification at constituency, regional, or ward level.
Holyrood national totals are aggregated by the Electoral
Management Board for Scotland.

#### Dispute resolution — the election petition

Per Representation of the People Act 1983 Part III, an
**election petition** may be brought to the **Election Court**
(Court of Session in Scotland) within 21 days of declaration.
Standing: any registered elector in the contest, or any
candidate. Grounds: corrupt or illegal practices, breach of
electoral law, or other irregularity.

The Election Court is a two-judge court (in Scotland, two
Court of Session judges) with statutory power to declare an
election void, declare another candidate elected, or order a
re-run.

There is **no separate electoral court** akin to Brazil's
*Justiça Eleitoral*; election disputes are heard within the
ordinary court system under specialised statutory procedure.

### F. Media access

Governed by the **Communications Act 2003** (UK) and **Ofcom
Broadcasting Code** (Section 6: Elections and Referendums).
Public service broadcasters (BBC, STV, Channel 4, Channel 5)
must provide due impartiality and provide a "**party election
broadcast**" allocation by formula. The formula weights
parties' representation and recent vote shares.

The Communications Act framework is UK-wide; there is no
Scotland-specific media-access statute for Scottish
Parliament elections. The BBC operates Scotland-specific
political broadcasting (BBC Scotland) which follows the Ofcom
framework.

### G. Districting law — captured under Component 1

District structure for Holyrood is set in Scotland Act 1998
Schedule 1, with periodic review by the **Boundary Commission
for Scotland** (a UK statutory body). District structure for
local government is set by the **Local Government Boundary
Commission for Scotland** (a Scottish statutory body),
operating under the Local Government (Boundaries) (Scotland)
Act 1973 and subsequent statutes. The two Boundary Commissions
are statutorily distinct and operate on different timetables.

## 6. PROSeS performance diagnostic

The empirical anchors are the **6 May 2021 Holyrood election**
and the **5 May 2022 Scottish local government elections** —
the most recent regular cycles for each regime. Sources:

- **Electoral Management Board for Scotland** — administrative
  data and post-election reports.
- **Electoral Commission** — UK-wide regulatory data and
  campaign-finance information.
- **National Records of Scotland** — population and demographic
  baseline.
- Academic literature on Scottish elections (notably the
  *Scottish Affairs* journal and the Centre on Constitutional
  Change).

### Process Design

**Public participation**: the Electoral Commission and the EMB
conduct public consultation on procedural amendments. The
**Scottish Parliament Standards, Procedures and Public
Appointments Committee** scrutinises Holyrood electoral
legislation; the **Local Government, Housing and Planning
Committee** scrutinises local-government electoral
legislation.

**Probity and impartiality**: no significant integrity
concerns in recent cycles. The Electoral Commission's annual
report consistently rates Scottish electoral administration
as compliant with statutory requirements.

**Accountability**: dispute resolution channels exist
(Election Court via Court of Session); the absence of a
specialised electoral court means dispute resolution shares
court time with other matters.

### Resource Investment

**Transparency**: party accounts published by Electoral
Commission; candidate spending returns published by Returning
Officers; election results published by the EMB and
Returning Officers.

**Sustainability**: the four-year electoral cycle for both
Holyrood and local government provides predictable
administrative planning. Statutory funding flows through the
relevant UK and Scottish departmental budgets.

**Legitimacy**: regulatory stability is high; the core
electoral provisions of the Scotland Act 1998 have been
substantively unchanged since 1998 (subject to periodic
boundary reviews and the 2016 transfer of competence). The
local-government regime has been stable since the 2007
introduction of STV.

### Service Output Quality

**Convenience**: in-person voting at polling stations on
election day; postal voting available on application; proxy
voting in defined circumstances. Voter ID is **not** required
for Scottish Parliament or local-government elections (this is
a divergence from the UK Elections Act 2022 which introduced
voter ID for Westminster elections in England, Wales, and
Northern Ireland; the Scottish Parliament has not introduced
analogous voter ID for devolved elections).

**Accuracy**: paper ballots used at all stages; manual count
for Holyrood; electronic count for local government with
certified software.

**Efficiency**: same-night declaration for Holyrood
constituency results; electronic STV count typically produces
local-government results within 24 hours of poll close.

### Service Outcomes

#### Voter turnout

- **Holyrood 2021**: 63.5% (constituency); 63.4% (regional).
  Highest Holyrood turnout since 1999.
- **Local government 2022**: 44.8% — modestly higher than the
  2017 figure (46.9%) but well below Holyrood turnout. The
  decoupling of local elections from Holyrood elections
  (introduced by the Scottish Local Government (Elections)
  Act 2009) produces consistently lower local turnout.

#### Invalid ballots

- **Holyrood 2021**: invalid-ballot rates of 0.4%
  (constituency) and 0.8% (regional) — the higher regional
  rate reflects the two-vote ballot complexity.
- **Local government 2022**: invalid-ballot rate of 0.6%
  across STV wards. Low by international comparison
  (comparable to Irish Dáil STV figures of 0.5–1.0%).

#### Representation outcomes

**Gender representation**:
- Holyrood 2021: 58 of 129 MSPs are women (45%).
- Local councils 2022: approximately 35% women councillors
  (varies by council; Edinburgh 40%, Glasgow 47%).

Both figures are substantially higher than pre-2007
baselines; the principal driver has been internal party
selection rules (Scottish Labour all-women shortlists, SNP
zipping) permitted by Equality Act 2010 s. 104.

**Ethnic minority representation**: the Scottish population
is approximately 4% non-white (2022 census); MSP and
councillor numbers from non-white backgrounds remain below
this share. The closed-list architecture at Holyrood places
the principal responsibility on party-internal selection;
the STV architecture at local level places the principal
responsibility on candidate emergence and voter ranking
behaviour. There is no statutory minority-representation
mechanism in either regime.

#### Outcomes for the project's three priorities

1. **Legibility of the law**:
   - Scotland Act 1998 is a single, accessible UK statute.
     Section 8 specifies the allocation formula procedurally
     in five sub-sections of statutory text. The 2015 Order
     implements the framework cleanly.
   - Local Governance (Scotland) Act 2004 is similarly
     concise (16 sections); the 2011 Order implements at the
     procedural level.
   - **The architecture is legibly two-track**: a Holyrood
     stack (1998 Act + 2015 Order) and a local stack (2004
     Act + 2011 Order), each free-standing.

2. **Interconnection of statutes**:
   - Cross-reference between the Holyrood stack and the local
     stack is **minimal**. The two regimes operate in
     parallel under separate Acts.
   - Cross-reference to UK statutes (RPA 1983, PPERA 2000,
     Equality Act 2010, Communications Act 2003) is
     extensive. The Scottish electoral architecture depends
     on UK statutory backdrop for franchise mechanics, party
     registration, candidate-diversity safe-harbour, and
     media regulation.
   - **The connected-area architecture is multi-layered**:
     UK Acts provide the framework; Scottish Acts provide
     the system-specific rules; Orders provide implementing
     detail.

3. **Capacity for minority representation**:
   - Holyrood: closed-list regional architecture concentrates
     responsibility in party-internal selection. The Equality
     Act 2010 s. 104 permissive framework enables but does
     not require diverse candidate selection.
   - Local: STV in M = 3/4 wards produces structural
     thresholds of 20–25%, which is achievable for
     geographically concentrated minorities but operates as a
     constraint for dispersed minorities. There is no
     statutory candidate-diversity provision specific to
     local elections.
   - Neither regime has a reserved-seats mechanism analogous
     to New Zealand's Māori electorates.

### Stakeholder Satisfaction

Generally stable. The principal ongoing reform debates
concern:
- Voter ID for Scottish elections (Scottish Government has
  resisted; Westminster has introduced voter ID for
  Westminster elections);
- The 8-region structure (whether to consolidate into fewer
  larger regions for greater proportionality, or to retain
  current geographic regions);
- The M = 3/4 STV ward band (whether to permit M = 5 or M =
  6 for greater proportionality);
- Voter access for non-British residents (the 2020 franchise
  expansion has been operationalised).

## 8. Gardner portability assessment for US implementation

| Provision | Type | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Two-thirds super-majority lock on core electoral provisions (Scotland Act 1998 s. 31(1A)) | **Universal** | The drafting device — statutorily entrenched super-majority requirement on specified provisions — is portable to any ordinary-statute regime. The procedural barrier produces de facto constitutional-grade stability without constitutional amendment. |
| Modified Jefferson/D'Hondt with constituency-correction divisor (Scotland Act 1998 s. 8) | **Universal** | The compensatory MMP allocation procedure is portable. Section 8's procedural drafting (without using the eponym) is also portable. |
| Sub-national regional list tier | **Hybrid** | The substance — list tier sized smaller than national — is portable. The Scottish 8-region structure reflects specific geographic choices that would not transfer; the underlying design parameter (regional vs. national list tier) is universal. |
| Statutory magnitude band (M = 3 or M = 4, Local Governance (Scotland) Act 2004 s. 1(3)(b)) | **Universal** | The single-sentence rule "each ward is to return three or four councillors" is directly portable. Acts as a structural threshold lock without specifying percentages. |
| Island-exception amendment (Islands (Scotland) Act 2018 s. 8) | **Hybrid** | The substance — boundary commissions may depart from the magnitude band in specified circumstances — is portable. The specific island-population rationale reflects Scottish geography. |
| Permissive candidate-diversity safe harbour (Equality Act 2010 s. 104) | **Hybrid** | The permissive model — statute allows party-internal diversity practices without mandating them — is portable in concept; US constitutional posture on the equivalent of party-internal sex-based selection rules differs. |
| No statutory percentage threshold | **Universal** | The choice to rely on structural threshold alone is portable. Documented effects are well understood. |
| No national leveling in MMP | **Particular** | The Scottish choice not to level at the national level reflects Scottish post-1998 design choices; for US drafters considering MMP, the choice between sub-national-tier-only and national-leveling is the key design question. |
| Electoral Management Board coordinating layer (Local Electoral Administration (Scotland) Act 2011) | **Universal** | The mid-layer EMB structure — coordinating across local Returning Officers without removing their statutory authority — is portable to US state contexts. |
| STV with Droop quota + WIGM surplus transfer (Scottish Local Government Elections Order 2011 Schedule 1) | **Hybrid** | STV-as-counting-protocol is portable; the specific WIGM software-certification regime reflects 2007 transition choices that other jurisdictions would re-evaluate. |
| Alphabetical-only ballot ordering (Scottish Local Government Elections Order 2011 Schedule 1 para. 21) | **Particular** | The Kerley Committee rejection of Robson rotation reflects 2000 administrative-complexity assessment; subsequent ATL-rotation experience in Tasmania and the ACT suggests this is a reconsidered choice. |
| Candidate "descriptions" registered via PPERA (Political Parties, Elections and Referendums Act 2000 Schedule 4) | **Hybrid** | The registration mechanism for party descriptions is portable; the absence of a non-party-group entity class is a Scottish-specific gap that US drafters working with non-partisan local elections would need to fill differently. |

### Top three takeaways for US state/local implementation

1. **Statutory super-majority entrenchment of core electoral
   provisions.** Scotland Act 1998 s. 31(1A) provides the
   project's clearest documented model of how a statute can
   produce constitutional-grade stability for electoral
   mechanics without requiring constitutional amendment. A US
   state statute that designates specified sections —
   assembly size, district structure, allocation formula — as
   amendable only by two-thirds super-majority would
   replicate the effect within an ordinary-statute regime.
   The Scottish architecture demonstrates that this drafting
   device is durable: the core 1998 provisions have not been
   substantively amended in the 25+ years since enactment.

2. **MMP with sub-national list tiers produces a distinct
   compensation profile.** The Scottish Holyrood regime
   demonstrates that an MMP design with regional-tier lists
   (rather than a single national-tier list) and no national
   leveling produces a less proportional outcome than the
   German federal or New Zealand models, while preserving
   geographic representation in the list tier. For US state-
   legislature MMP designs, the Scottish architecture is the
   documented working case for "MMP with regional list tiers
   of approximately 7 seats each." The trade-offs are
   measurable: residual constituency-tier disproportionality
   is preserved in chamber outcomes; small parties face a
   higher effective threshold (12.5% per region structural)
   than in national-list MMP systems.

3. **Statutory magnitude bands as a structural-threshold
   lock.** The Local Governance (Scotland) Act 2004 s.
   1(3)(b) rule — "each ward is to return three or four
   councillors" — is a single-sentence drafting device that
   sets the structural threshold of exclusion at 20–25% for
   every ward in every council without specifying a
   percentage threshold anywhere in the statute. For US
   drafters of multi-member-ward local elections, the
   magnitude-band rule is directly importable. The 2018
   island-exception amendment shows how the band can be
   relaxed in specifically defined circumstances without
   undermining the general rule.
