# Spain — Congress and Municipal Elections (LOREG)

## 0. Case metadata

- **Country**: Kingdom of Spain (Reino de España)
- **Sub-jurisdiction**: National (Congreso de los Diputados) and
  local (*ayuntamientos*, ~8,131 municipalities). Sub-national
  electoral law for the 17 autonomous communities is set by each
  community's own electoral law, supplementing LOREG.
- **Levels of government covered**: This profile covers the
  national Congress and municipal elections — both governed by
  a single comprehensive statute (LOREG). The 17 autonomous-
  community electoral regimes are noted but not separately
  documented.
- **Electoral system family**: **Closed-list PR** at all levels.
  D'Hondt allocation. Per-district 3% threshold for Congress;
  5% for municipal. Single mark for one list per voter; no
  intra-list voter agency.
- **Governing statutes**:
  - **Ley Orgánica 5/1985, de 19 de junio, del Régimen
    Electoral General** (LOREG) — the comprehensive statute
    for all electoral processes governed by national law.
    Last amended Ley Orgánica 1/2025 (effective 23 January
    2025).
  - **Constitución Española (1978)**, Arts. 23 (participation),
    14 (equality), 68 (Congress), 69 (Senate), 140
    (municipalities).
  - **Junta Electoral Central** (JEC) **Instructions** —
    binding administrative interpretations of LOREG.
- **Implementing instruments**: JEC and provincial *Juntas
  Electorales* issue cycle-specific instructions and resolve
  disputes.
- **Last regular elections**: Municipal — **28 May 2023**.
  General — **23 July 2023**.
- **Analyst**: this profile, 2026-05-05.

### Why this case is in the project

Three features make Spain the project's case for **statutory
legibility and interconnection** in PR law:

1. **A single comprehensive statute** covers all electoral
   levels. LOREG's Título I sets common provisions; subsequent
   títulos handle specific levels (Congress, Senate, municipal,
   Cabildos, Provincial Deputies, European Parliament). For a
   U.S. drafter, LOREG is the project's clearest example of a
   *unified electoral code* approach as an alternative to
   level-specific statutes.
2. **The statute-instruction-jurisprudence cascade** is
   well-articulated. Constitution Art. 23 sets the right;
   LOREG sets the rules; JEC instructions resolve operational
   ambiguity; Tribunal Constitucional jurisprudence refines
   constitutional interpretation. Each layer cross-references
   the others.
3. ***Agrupación de electores*** — the project's principal
   case for a non-party-list mechanism inside a partisan
   national framework. Article 44 LOREG defines voter
   groupings with population-tiered signature requirements;
   Articles 169 and 187 govern submission for general and
   municipal elections respectively.

For minority-representation analysis, Spain documents two
distinct patterns: **regional minorities** (Catalan, Basque,
Galician, Aragonese) are accommodated through the per-province
D'Hondt structure of Congress elections; **ethnic minorities**
(notably the ~700,000-strong Roma/gitano community) have
minimal representation under the same structure.

## 1. Statutory architecture — the LOREG framework

LOREG is organized into 240+ articles across nine títulos. The
relevant structure:

| Título | Subject | Articles |
|---|---|---|
| **Preliminar** | Object and scope of the law | Arts. 1–2 |
| **Título I** | *Disposiciones comunes para las elecciones por sufragio universal directo* — common provisions for all elections | Arts. 3–153 |
| **Título II** | *Disposiciones especiales para las elecciones de Diputados y Senadores* — Congress and Senate | Arts. 154–209 |
| **Título III** | *Disposiciones especiales para las elecciones municipales* — municipal elections | Arts. 179–209 |
| **Título IV** | *Disposiciones especiales para la elección de Cabildos Insulares Canarios* — Canary Island Councils | Arts. 201–209 |
| **Título V** | *Disposiciones especiales para la elección de Diputados Provinciales* — Provincial Deputies | Arts. 204–209 |
| **Título VI** | *Disposiciones especiales para las elecciones al Parlamento Europeo* — European Parliament | Arts. 210–227 |
| **Título VII** | (Repealed) | — |
| **Título VIII** | (Repealed) | — |
| Disposiciones adicionales / transitorias / finales | Additional, transitional, final provisions | — |

The Título-I-then-level-specific structure means common
provisions (administration, suffrage, candidacies, ballot
papers, dispute resolution, electoral offences) apply across
all levels; level-specific provisions add or modify only what
must differ. **A change to the common provisions in Título I
ripples through every level simultaneously**.

### Statute-instruction-jurisprudence cascade

Operational interpretation of LOREG runs through three layers:

1. **Constitution Art. 23**: the constitutional right of
   participation (a fundamental right, *recurso de amparo*
   reviewable). Tribunal Constitucional jurisprudence sets the
   constitutional baseline.
2. **LOREG**: the operational rules. As a *Ley Orgánica*, LOREG
   amendments require absolute majority of Congress (rather
   than simple majority). This places a structural barrier
   against majoritarian erosion.
3. **JEC Instructions** (*Instrucciones de la Junta Electoral
   Central*) — binding administrative interpretations
   resolving operational ambiguity. JEC instructions are
   numbered, dated, and formally promulgated; they create a
   cumulative body of precedent. (Example: JEC Instruction
   7/2011 on signature-authentication procedure for Congress
   candidacies, interpreting LOREG Arts. 169 and 220.)

**For U.S. drafters**: this layered architecture is the
project's most directly imitable model of how electoral law
can be made legible. Each operational question has a known
home — Constitution for fundamental rights; LOREG article for
the rule; JEC instruction for the procedure. Cross-references
within LOREG are explicit (Arts. 169 and 220 are both
explicitly referenced from JEC Instruction 7/2011).

## 2. Component 1 — Seat Product (Assembly Size × District Magnitude)

### Congress (Title II, LOREG Arts. 154–168)

Per Constitution Art. 68(2) and LOREG Arts. 161–162:

- **Total seats**: **350** (Constitution sets a range of 300–400;
  LOREG Art. 162 fixes the count at 350).
- **District structure**: each of the **50 provinces** is a
  district, plus the autonomous cities of **Ceuta** (1 seat)
  and **Melilla** (1 seat), for **52 districts** total.
- **Per-district magnitude**: each province receives a
  **minimum of 2 seats**; the remaining 248 seats are
  distributed proportionally to population.

Resulting magnitudes (from the most recent assignment):
- Smallest provinces (Soria, Teruel, Cuenca): M = 2.
- Largest provinces (Madrid, Barcelona): M = 37 and M = 32.
- Ceuta and Melilla: M = 1 each.

### Municipal elections (Título III, LOREG Arts. 179–198)

Per LOREG Art. 179, council size scales with population on a
fixed table. Each municipality is a single electoral district;
M = total council size.

| Population | Council size |
|---|---:|
| ≤ 100 | open-list assembly (concejo abierto) |
| 101 – 250 | 5 |
| 251 – 1,000 | 7 |
| 1,001 – 2,000 | 9 |
| 2,001 – 5,000 | 11 |
| 5,001 – 10,000 | 13 |
| 10,001 – 20,000 | 17 |
| 20,001 – 50,000 | 21 |
| 50,001 – 100,000 | 25 |

For municipalities >100,000, the council adds 1 seat per
100,000 inhabitants (or fraction), with one additional seat if
the result is even, ensuring an odd-numbered council. The
largest municipal councils (Madrid, Barcelona) have 57 and 41
councilors respectively.


### Notes — minority-representation implications of the seat product

The structural feature most directly relevant to minority
representation is the **per-province minimum of 2 seats** under
Constitution Art. 68(2). This produces M = 2 in many small
rural provinces, with corresponding 33% structural threshold
of exclusion. Practical effect: only the two largest parties
in those provinces can win representation.

This systematically disadvantages small parties whose support
is geographically dispersed (rather than concentrated in one
province). Regional minority parties (Catalan, Basque,
Galician) are well-served by the structure — they concentrate
in specific provinces and clear the local threshold easily.
Ethnic and religious minorities (Roma, Muslim, recent migrant
communities) are dispersed and accordingly underrepresented.

For U.S. drafters: the Spanish Congress structure is a working
illustration that **per-district minimum-seat allocation
combined with low-magnitude rural districts produces strong
exclusionary effects on geographically-dispersed minorities**.
A U.S. PR adoption that retains rural-skewed representation
through district-minimum rules will have analogous
distributional consequences.

## 3. Component 2 — Ballot Structure

### Closed list (Arts. 96, 169, 188 LOREG)

At all levels covered by this profile, voters cast **a single
mark for one list**. Lists are submitted in the order set by
the submitter; voters cannot:

- reorder candidates (no preference vote);
- cumulate;
- panachage;
- cast a separate party-only vote (no equivalent of Brazilian
  *voto de legenda*; the single mark is functionally a list
  vote, with all candidates of the list ranked in submitter-
  set order).

### Operationalized values

```

                                 no intra-list ordering effect
panachage_allowed:               no

                                 Ley Orgánica 6/2002 (Partidos
                                 Políticos)

                                 electores* under LOREG Arts.
                                 44, 169 (Congress), 187
                                 (municipal)
voter_grouping_legal_term:       agrupación de electores
citizen_committee_lists_allowed: yes

                                 by LO 3/2007): "composición

```

### Notes — minority-representation implications of ballot architecture

The closed list with binding submitter order means **the
party / agrupación selecting the list determines the full
candidate identity** that voters can support. Voters' only
choice is which list to vote for; the candidate composition is
fixed by the submitter.

For minority representation, this places enormous weight on
the *internal candidate-selection processes* of parties and
agrupaciones de electores. A party's commitment to including
minority candidates in winnable list positions (i.e., near the
top of its list) determines whether minority representation
results.

The 2007 *Ley Orgánica 3/2007* gender-quota provision (LOREG
Art. 44 bis) requires "balanced composition" — minimum 40% of
each sex — on lists of 5+ candidates, and on every successive
section of 5 positions within a list. This statutory rule
produced measurable increases in women's candidacy and
representation at the Congress and municipal levels.

The same statutory mechanism has *not* been extended to
ethnic-minority candidacies. There is no analogous quota for
Roma, migrant-origin, or other ethnic-minority candidates.
Tribunal Constitucional has not addressed analogous claims
under Article 14 or Article 23.

For U.S. drafters: the Spanish gender-quota mechanism (LOREG
Art. 44 bis) is a portable model for *statutory list
composition requirements*. The U.S. constitutional posture on
quotas is more constrained than Spain's, but the technical
drafting of the rule — defining the quota in terms of
"sections of 5 positions" rather than overall list percentages
— is a directly importable approach.

## 4. Component 3 — Allocation Formula

### D'Hondt with thresholds (LOREG Arts. 163, 180)

Per LOREG Art. 163 (Congress) and Art. 180 (municipal), seats
are allocated by the **D'Hondt highest-averages method** within
each district.

#### Article 163 (Congress) — operative provisions

- **Threshold**: 3% of valid votes within the district.
- **Formula**: D'Hondt — vote totals divided successively by
  1, 2, 3, 4, … ; seats awarded by descending order of
  resulting quotients.
- **Ties**: in event of equal averages, the seat goes to the
  list with the higher absolute vote total; further ties
  resolved by lot.

#### Article 180 (municipal) — operative provisions

- **Threshold**: 5% of valid votes within the municipality.
- **Formula**: D'Hondt — same as Congress.
- **Ties**: same as Congress.

The 5% municipal threshold is higher than the 3% Congress
threshold; the policy rationale (per Art. 180 legislative
history) was to discourage municipal-council fragmentation.

### Notes — minority-representation implications of allocation

D'Hondt's structural large-list bias compounds the per-province
minimum-seat structural effect at low magnitudes (Component 1).
In a 5-seat province, D'Hondt reliably awards all five seats
to the top two or three parties; parties below 15–20% of votes
typically receive none.

The 3% Congress threshold has limited additional effect because
the structural threshold of exclusion in most provinces exceeds
3%. The 5% municipal threshold has more effect: in a 13-seat
council (M = 13, structural threshold 7.1%), the 5% legal
threshold is non-binding, but in larger councils (M = 25, M =
57), the 5% threshold is the operative constraint.

For minority representation specifically:

- **Regional minorities**: well-accommodated. Catalan, Basque,
  and Galician parties win representation in their home
  provinces; the per-province D'Hondt structure is in fact a
  feature, not a bug, for regional concentration.
- **Ethnic / religious minorities (Roma, Muslim, migrant
  communities)**: poorly served. Geographic dispersion + low-
  magnitude per-province structure + closed-list submitter
  selection together produce minimal representation.
- **Migrant-origin candidates**: the 2007 gender-quota law
  has driven female candidacy increases, but the same
  mechanism has not been extended to migrant-origin or
  ethnic-minority candidates.

For U.S. drafters: the Spanish experience documents that
**proportional representation alone does not produce
representation of dispersed minorities**. The Spanish
Constitutional framework requires per-province minimum-seat
allocation (Art. 68(2)); under that constraint, formula and
threshold choices produce only marginal effects on dispersed-
minority representation. The U.S. analog would be a state-
constitutional or charter requirement that allocates seats
disproportionately to rural areas; in that situation, even a
generous PR formula will struggle to deliver representation for
geographically-dispersed minorities.

## 5. Connected areas — emphasizing inter-statute connections

### A. Voter eligibility (LOREG Arts. 2–3)

- **Congress**: Spanish citizens 18+ with electoral inscription
  (LOREG Art. 2).
- **Municipal**: Spanish citizens; EU citizens (Maastricht
  obligation, transposed by LOREG Art. 176); residents of
  countries with reciprocal arrangements (currently 12
  countries). Voting age 18+.

The franchise extension to non-Spanish residents at municipal
level is a *cross-statute connection*: LOREG Art. 176 explicitly
incorporates EU Treaty obligations and Spanish bilateral
treaties, with the JEC maintaining a current list of reciprocal
countries.

### B. Candidate / list registration

#### Statutory text — Article 44 LOREG (eligible list-bearers)

> Pueden presentar candidatos o listas de candidatos:
>
> a) Los partidos y federaciones inscritos en el Registro
>    correspondiente.
>
> b) Las coaliciones constituidas según lo dispuesto en el
>    apartado 2 de este artículo.
>
> c) Las agrupaciones de electores que reúnan los requisitos
>    establecidos por las disposiciones especiales de la
>    presente Ley.

(Translation: The following may present candidates or lists of
candidates: (a) parties and federations registered in the
appropriate Register; (b) coalitions constituted under the
provisions of paragraph 2 of this article; (c) voter groupings
meeting the requirements established by the special provisions
of this law.)

#### Article 187 (municipal) — agrupaciones de electores signature requirements

For municipal elections, agrupaciones de electores must collect
signatures from registered voters in the municipal census
(authenticated notarially or by the municipal Secretary). The
schedule scales with population:

| Population | Signatures required |
|---|---:|
| < 5,000 | 1% of registered voters (minimum 2× number of councilors) |
| 5,001 – 10,000 | 100 |
| 10,001 – 50,000 | 500 |
| 50,001 – 150,000 | 1,500 |
| 150,001 – 300,000 | 3,000 |
| 300,001 – 1,000,000 | 5,000 |
| > 1,000,000 | 8,000 |

A voter may sign the supporting list of **only one
candidacy** (LOREG Art. 187(2)) — paralleling the Bavarian
Art. 28 GLKrWG single-list-only rule.

#### Inter-statute connection — Ley Orgánica 6/2002 de Partidos Políticos

Party registration (LOREG Art. 44(a)) operates through Ley
Orgánica 6/2002 *de Partidos Políticos* — a separate statute
governing party formation, internal democracy, financing
disclosure, and grounds for dissolution. LOREG Art. 44 *cross-
references the Register of Political Parties*; the Register
itself is constituted under LO 6/2002.

This is an example of **statutory layering**: LOREG sets the
electoral rule but defers party-status questions to the
specialized statute. The cascade is:

- **Constitution Art. 6**: parties as fundamental democratic
  instruments.
- **LO 6/2002**: party registration, internal organization,
  dissolution.
- **LOREG Art. 44**: electoral participation through registered
  parties or their alternatives.

For U.S. drafters: this layered architecture suggests that a
U.S. PR statute can defer specific party-status questions to a
specialized parties-law (where one exists, such as the Federal
Election Campaign Act for parties, or state-level statutes) by
*explicit cross-reference* in the electoral statute.

### C. Campaign finance — *Ley Orgánica 8/2007*

Campaign finance is governed by **Ley Orgánica 8/2007 de
Financiación de los Partidos Políticos**, separate from LOREG.
LOREG Articles 121–134 cover *gastos electorales y subvenciones*
(electoral expenses and subsidies), with cross-references to LO
8/2007 for substantive financing rules. The two statutes
operate in parallel: LOREG sets the electoral cycle; LO 8/2007
sets the year-round financing regime.

### D. Election administration — the Junta Electoral Central

LOREG Articles 8–22 establish the **Junta Electoral Central**
(JEC) and the layered electoral administration:

- **Junta Electoral Central** (JEC): national, 13 members
  (8 Supreme Court magistrates, 5 academics).
- **Juntas Electorales Provinciales**: one per province.
- **Juntas Electorales de Zona**: regional sub-units.
- **Mesas electorales**: polling-station boards.

JEC issues binding **Instrucciones** that interpret LOREG
provisions. The instructions are numbered, dated, and
publicly available through the JEC website. They constitute
a body of administrative precedent that complements judicial
interpretation of LOREG.

For U.S. drafters: the JEC instruction layer is an
analytically useful model for a binding administrative-
interpretation regime. The U.S. analog would be FEC advisory
opinions or state Secretary of State interpretive memoranda.
The Spanish model is more formally structured: JEC
instructions are numbered, dated, cite specific LOREG
articles, and are reviewable by the Tribunal Supremo on
administrative-law grounds.

### E. Election dispute resolution

Per LOREG Article 109 et seq., electoral disputes run through:
- **Junta Electoral Central** for administrative matters at
  the national level;
- **Tribunal Supremo, Sala de lo Contencioso-Administrativo**
  for review of JEC decisions;
- **Tribunal Constitucional** via *recurso de amparo* for
  constitutional questions affecting Article 23 (right of
  participation) or Article 14 (equality).

The cascade: JEC → Tribunal Supremo → Tribunal Constitucional.
LOREG Article 49 defines the timing and standing rules for
electoral *recurso contencioso-electoral*.

### F. Regional electoral laws (autonomous communities)

Each of the 17 autonomous communities has its own electoral
law for community-level elections. These laws supplement
LOREG for community-specific provisions. Example: Catalan
electoral law (Llei 13/2020), Basque electoral law (Ley
5/1990).

The autonomous-community electoral laws **explicitly
incorporate LOREG** for matters not separately regulated. Each
community statute begins with a clause along the lines of
"For matters not regulated by this Law, LOREG applies
supplementarily." This produces a direct statutory hierarchy:

- **Constitution** (national): fundamental rights and
  framework.
- **LOREG** (national, organic): comprehensive electoral
  regime.
- **Community electoral law** (autonomic, organic for some
  communities): community-level supplementation.

### G. (Districting law — captured under Component 1)

District structure for Congress is fixed in Constitution Art.
68(2) (50-province + Ceuta + Melilla); for municipal, each
municipality is its own district (no sub-municipal divisions).

## 6. PROSeS performance diagnostic — minority-representation focus

**Empirical anchors**: the **23 July 2023 general election** and
the **28 May 2023 municipal elections** are the most recent
cycles. Sources:

- *Junta Electoral Central* (JEC) — official results.
- *Instituto Nacional de Estadística* (INE) — voter and
  population data.
- Academic literature on minority representation in Spain
  (notably the *Revista Española de Investigaciones
  Sociológicas*).

### Service Outcomes — minority representation

#### Regional / linguistic minority representation

Catalan, Basque, Galician, and other regionally-concentrated
minority parties are well-represented in Congress. In the 2023
general election:

- ERC (Catalan left-republican): 7 seats.
- PNV (Basque nationalist): 5 seats.
- EH Bildu (Basque left-independentist): 6 seats.
- BNG (Galician nationalist): 1 seat.
- Junts (Catalan center-right independentist): 7 seats.
- Coalición Canaria: 1 seat.

Total regional / linguistic-minority parties: ~27 seats out of
350 (~7.7%). Roughly proportional to the share of voters in
the relevant communities.

#### Ethnic-minority representation — the Roma/gitano gap

The Roma/gitano population in Spain is approximately 700,000–
1,100,000 (~1.5–2.3% of total population), making it the
largest ethnic minority in Spain by number. Roma/gitano
representation in Congress and at municipal level has been
**minimal across all cycles since 1978**:

- Congress: zero Roma/gitano deputies elected in any cycle.
- Municipal: a small number of Roma/gitano councilors
  elected, primarily in municipalities with substantial Roma
  populations (~50 council seats nationally in 2023, against
  a 700,000+ population).

The structural causes are the closed-list submitter selection
(Component 2) and the geographic dispersion of the Roma
population (Component 1 / 3).

For U.S. drafters: the Spanish case is a documented negative
precedent for minority-representation outcomes under
proportional representation absent specific statutory
mechanisms (such as reserved seats, candidate-quota
provisions, or lower district magnitudes that would permit
geographic concentration to translate into representation).

#### Gender representation — LOREG Art. 44 bis

The 2007 gender-quota provision (LOREG Art. 44 bis) requires
"balanced composition" — minimum 40% of each sex — on lists
of ≥ 5 candidates. The provision applies in sections of 5
positions: each section of 5 (positions 1–5, 6–10, etc.) must
have at least 2 of each sex.

**Operational effect**: in the 23 July 2023 Congress election,
women won 154 of 350 seats (44%); in 2023 municipal elections,
women hold approximately 40% of council seats nationally. Both
are substantially higher than pre-2007 baselines.

### Service Output Quality

Spanish electoral administration is consistently rated highly.
Invalid-vote rates at the 2023 general election were ~0.7%;
turnout at 70.4% (general) and 64.5% (municipal).

### Stakeholder Satisfaction

Generally high. The 2007 gender quota and the 2011 reform of
non-resident voting (the *voto rogado* requirement, partially
reformed in 2022) have been the principal substantive reform
debates of the past two decades; both have produced
significant academic and political-commentary engagement.

## 8. Gardner portability assessment for US implementation

| Provision | Type | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Single comprehensive electoral statute (LOREG) | **Universal** | The unified-code approach is portable; reduces fragmentation across levels and simplifies cross-reference. |
| Statute-instruction-jurisprudence cascade | **Universal** | The layered architecture (Constitution → LOREG → JEC instruction → Tribunal Constitucional) is a portable model for legibility. |
| Agrupación de electores with population-tiered signatures (Art. 187) | **Universal** | Drafting model for non-party-list ballot access at municipal level. The schedule scales cleanly. |
| Single-list-only signature rule (Art. 187(2)) | **Universal** | Same as Bavarian Art. 28 GLKrWG; portable. |
| LO 6/2002 cross-reference for party-status questions | **Universal** | The deferral pattern (electoral statute → party statute) is portable. |
| Per-province minimum-seat allocation (Const. Art. 68(2)) | **Particular** | Reflects Spanish constitutional federalism; minimum-seat allocation produces strong exclusionary effects on dispersed minorities, a U.S. PR adoption should anticipate. |
| LOREG Art. 44 bis gender quota (40% balanced composition in sections of 5) | **Hybrid** | The technical drafting is portable; U.S. constitutional posture on quotas more constrained. |
| 5% municipal threshold | **Universal** | Standard parameter; tunable. |

### Top three takeaways for US state/local implementation

1. **A single unified electoral statute is more legible than
   level-specific statutes.** LOREG's structure — common
   provisions in Título I, level-specific provisions in
   Títulos II–VI — is the project's clearest model. A U.S.
   state statute that consolidates all electoral provisions
   (state legislature, county, municipal, school board) under
   a single code with common provisions would significantly
   reduce drafting and litigation complexity relative to the
   level-by-level fragmentation typical in U.S. state
   electoral law.

2. **The statute-instruction-jurisprudence cascade reduces
   operational ambiguity.** Spain's JEC instructions
   constitute a public, numbered, dated body of binding
   administrative precedent that interprets LOREG provisions.
   For U.S. drafters: a state Secretary of State or election
   commissioner equivalent could be statutorily empowered to
   issue analogous numbered instructions. The combination of
   statute + binding instructions + judicial review produces
   substantially fewer operational disputes than statute +
   judicial review alone.

3. **Proportional representation alone does not produce
   representation of dispersed minorities.** The Spanish
   experience documents that PR with closed-list submitter
   selection, low-magnitude rural districts, and per-district
   D'Hondt allocation does not produce meaningful Roma /
   gitano representation despite ~2% of population. For U.S.
   PR-reform proposals with minority-representation
   objectives, the structural lesson is that the formula and
   threshold are not enough; the *list composition rules*
   (analogous to LOREG Art. 44 bis for gender) and the
   *district structure* (which determines whether minority
   geographic concentrations can translate to representation)
   must be addressed simultaneously.
