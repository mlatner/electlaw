# South Africa — National Assembly, Provincial Legislatures, and Municipal Councils

## 0. Case metadata

- **Country**: Republic of South Africa
- **Sub-jurisdiction**: National Assembly; nine provincial legislatures
  (Eastern Cape, Free State, Gauteng, KwaZulu-Natal, Limpopo,
  Mpumalanga, Northern Cape, North West, Western Cape); 257
  municipal councils (metropolitan, local, and district).
- **Levels of government covered**: All three levels — national,
  provincial, and local — under an integrated set of statutes
  administered by a single national electoral management body
  (the Electoral Commission of South Africa).
- **Electoral system family**:
  - **National Assembly**: closed-list proportional representation
    with a two-tier structure (200 seats from a national list /
    200 seats from nine provincial-to-national regional lists).
    The *Electoral Amendment Act 1 of 2023* added an independent-
    candidate channel; the system retains list-PR architecture for
    party contestants.
  - **Provincial legislatures**: closed-list proportional
    representation within each province as a single district.
  - **Municipal councils**: **mixed system** — half the seats from
    single-member wards on a first-past-the-post (FPTP) basis,
    half from closed party (or independent) lists, with a
    compensatory PR top-up calculation that produces overall
    proportional council composition.
- **Governing statutes**:
  - **Constitution of the Republic of South Africa, 1996**
    (Act 108 of 1996), Chapter 4 (Parliament), Chapter 6
    (Provinces), Chapter 7 (Local Government), Schedule 1A
    (allocation of provincial seats to the National Assembly,
    inserted by the *Electoral Laws Amendment Act 34 of 2003*),
    Schedule 3 Part A (election of National Assembly and
    provincial legislatures).
    <https://www.gov.za/documents/constitution-republic-south-africa-1996>
  - **Electoral Act 73 of 1998** (as amended through 2023). The
    operative national statute for National Assembly and
    provincial legislature elections; Schedule 1A sets the seat-
    allocation formula.
    <https://www.gov.za/documents/electoral-act>
  - **Electoral Amendment Act 1 of 2023** (assented 17 April
    2023). Introduced statutory access for independent
    candidates following the Constitutional Court ruling in
    *New Nation Movement NPC v President of the Republic of
    South Africa* [2020] ZACC 11. Amended Schedule 1A of the
    Electoral Act.
    <https://www.gov.za/documents/electoral-amendment-act-1-2023>
  - **Local Government: Municipal Structures Act 117 of 1998**.
    Defines council categories (metropolitan, local, district),
    determines council size, and sets the mixed electoral system
    in Schedule 1 (proportional component) and Schedule 2 (ward
    component). Most heavily amended of the local-government
    statutes.
    <https://www.gov.za/documents/local-government-municipal-structures-act>
  - **Local Government: Municipal Electoral Act 27 of 2000**.
    The operative election-administration statute for municipal
    elections — candidate nomination, voting, counting, and seat
    allocation per Item 5 of Schedule 1 to Act 117 of 1998.
    <https://www.gov.za/documents/local-government-municipal-electoral-act>
  - **Electoral Commission Act 51 of 1996**. Establishes the
    Independent Electoral Commission (IEC) — properly the
    *Electoral Commission of South Africa* — and its powers.
    <https://www.gov.za/documents/electoral-commission-act>
  - **Local Government: Municipal Demarcation Act 27 of 1998**.
    Establishes the Municipal Demarcation Board and the ward-
    delimitation framework.
    <https://www.gov.za/documents/local-government-municipal-demarcation-act>
  - **Political Party Funding Act 6 of 2018** (in force 1 April
    2021). Imposes public-funding allocation, private-donation
    disclosure, and prohibition rules on parties represented in
    Parliament or provincial legislatures.
    <https://www.gov.za/documents/political-party-funding-act>
- **Constitutional anchor**: 1996 Constitution sections 1 (founding
  values), 9 (equality), 19 (political rights), 46 (composition of
  National Assembly), 105 (composition of provincial legislatures),
  157 (composition of municipal councils), 190 (Electoral
  Commission), and Schedules 1A and 3.
- **Election Management Body**: **Electoral Commission of South
  Africa (IEC)** — a Chapter 9 institution under Constitution s 190,
  constituted under the Electoral Commission Act 51 of 1996.
- **Last regular elections**:
  - **National and provincial**: 29 May 2024 (the first cycle
    conducted under the 2023 reform).
  - **Local government**: 1 November 2021 (the next cycle is due
    in 2026).
- **Analyst**: this profile, 2026-05-15.

### Why this case is in the project

South Africa anchors three distinct project priorities:

1. **The municipal mixed system is the closest existing real-world
   analog to the project's preferred ballot-design baseline.** Half
   the council is elected from single-member wards on a FPTP basis;
   half from closed PR lists; the PR allocation operates as a
   compensatory top-up so that the council's composition tracks the
   overall vote share of each party / independent. The architecture
   is set in Schedule 1 (proportional component) and Schedule 2
   (ward component) of the *Municipal Structures Act 117 of 1998*,
   with the operational rules in the *Municipal Electoral Act 27
   of 2000*. The system handles partial-winner and overhang
   problems through a statutory formula on the face of the schedule
   — a feature of particular interest for U.S. drafters.

2. **The 2023 reform admitting independent candidates onto a
   national-level list-PR ballot** is the project's most recent
   primary-source case of a Constitutional Court compelling
   statutory change to a list-PR framework on fundamental-rights
   grounds. *New Nation Movement NPC v President of the Republic
   of South Africa* [2020] ZACC 11 held that the requirement of
   party membership for candidacy in National Assembly and
   provincial-legislature elections is inconsistent with section
   19(3)(b) of the Constitution. The *Electoral Amendment Act 1
   of 2023* operationalised that ruling and is the natural
   comparator for any U.S. PR proposal that must accommodate
   independents within a list framework.

3. **The constitutional commitment to substantive redress**
   (Constitution sections 1, 9, and the Preamble) makes South
   Africa the project's case for minority-representation
   architecture *as a constitutional baseline*. Unlike Spain
   (where the Constitution is silent on substantive representation
   of historically excluded groups), South Africa's 1996
   Constitution explicitly establishes equality and redress as
   founding values. This is consequential for the project's third
   analytical priority — capacity for minority representation —
   because it gives statutory drafters a constitutional mandate
   that other jurisdictions do not have.

## 1. Statutory architecture — how the South African scheme is organised

The South African electoral framework is unusual in the project's
case set in two respects:

- **All electoral law sits below the Constitution** in a tightly
  coupled set of statutes, with the Constitution itself supplying
  the formula and seat-allocation provisions in Schedules 1A and
  3. This produces a statute-constitution interconnection
  comparable to (but more substantial than) the Spanish
  Constitution / LOREG cascade.
- **Local-government electoral law is split between two statutes**:
  the *Municipal Structures Act 117 of 1998* sets the *architecture*
  (council size, mixed-system structure, allocation formula in
  Schedule 1); the *Municipal Electoral Act 27 of 2000* sets the
  *administrative procedures* (registration, nominations, voting,
  counting). The split parallels Brazil's *Código Eleitoral* /
  *Lei das Eleições* arrangement.

### The relevant schedules and their roles

| Instrument | Subject | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Constitution Schedule 1A | Determination of delegates to National Assembly from provinces; provincial-to-national seat allocation | Inserted by Act 34 of 2003; further amended 2023 |
| Constitution Schedule 3 Part A | Election procedures (National Assembly, provincial legislatures) — formula, ties, vacancies | Constitution as adopted 1996 |
| Electoral Act 73 of 1998, Schedules 1, 1A | Allocation of seats — National Assembly and provincial legislatures | Schedule 1A inserted/replaced 2023 |
| Municipal Structures Act 117 of 1998, Schedule 1 | PR component of municipal councils | Original 1998 |
| Municipal Structures Act 117 of 1998, Schedule 2 | Ward-and-PR mixed component | Original 1998 |
| Municipal Electoral Act 27 of 2000 | Operational procedures for municipal elections | Original 2000; multiple amendments |

### Statute-constitution-jurisprudence cascade

Three layers, analogous to the Spanish LOREG cascade:

1. **Constitution** sets the right (section 19) and the structural
   parameters (sections 46, 105, 157, Schedule 1A, Schedule 3
   Part A).
2. **Statutes** (Electoral Act, Municipal Structures Act, Municipal
   Electoral Act) implement the structural parameters and supply
   administrative rules.
3. **IEC regulations and Constitutional Court jurisprudence**
   refine operational interpretation. The Constitutional Court has
   been actively engaged with the electoral framework — most
   recently in *New Nation Movement* (2020) and *Independent
   Candidate Association v President of the Republic of South
   Africa* [2024] ZACC 21 (post-2024-election review of the 2023
   reform).

## 2. Component 1 — Seat Product (Assembly Size × District Magnitude)

### 2.1 National Assembly

#### Constitutional text — section 46(1)

> 46. (1) The National Assembly consists of no fewer than 350 and
> no more than 400 women and men elected as members in terms of an
> electoral system that —
>
> (a) is prescribed by national legislation;
>
> (b) is based on the national common voters roll;
>
> (c) provides for a minimum voting age of 18 years; and
>
> (d) results, in general, in proportional representation.

The number of seats has been fixed at **400** by national
legislation (Electoral Act 73 of 1998, read with Constitution
Schedule 1A as amended). Constitution s 46(1)(d) is the
constitutional commitment to "proportional representation, in
general" — a phrase that allows mixed and compensatory mechanics
but forecloses pure majoritarian designs.

#### Tier structure (Constitution Schedule 1A; Electoral Act Schedule 1A)

The National Assembly's 400 seats are allocated across **two
tiers**:

- **National tier (200 seats)**: filled from a national list
  submitted by each contesting party or, since 2023, from a
  national list permitted to independent candidates within the
  limits set by Schedule 1A of the Electoral Act.
- **Regional tier (200 seats)**: filled from nine provincial-to-
  national regional lists. Each province is allocated a fixed
  number of National Assembly seats per Constitution Schedule 1A.

The Schedule 1A allocation of regional seats to provinces is
weighted by registered voters in each province, recalculated for
each election. Approximate allocations (subject to election-cycle
recalculation):

| Province | Regional National Assembly seats (approx.) |
|---|---:|
| Eastern Cape | 24 |
| Free State | 11 |
| Gauteng | 47 |
| KwaZulu-Natal | 41 |
| Limpopo | 19 |
| Mpumalanga | 15 |
| Northern Cape | 5 |
| North West | 14 |
| Western Cape | 24 |
| **Total regional** | **200** |

(Exact numbers vary by cycle and are formally promulgated by the
IEC ahead of each election. Verbatim Schedule 1A text on the
recalculation formula is a primary-source gap, flagged.)

### 2.2 Provincial legislatures

#### Constitutional text — section 105(2)

Constitution s 105(2) sets each provincial legislature's size at
**not fewer than 30 and not more than 80**:

> A provincial legislature consists of women and men elected as
> members in terms of an electoral system that —
>
> (a) is prescribed by national legislation;
>
> (b) is based on that province's segment of the national common
> voters roll;
>
> (c) provides for a minimum voting age of 18 years; and
>
> (d) results, in general, in proportional representation.

Per Constitution s 105(2) and the Determination of the Number of
Members of Provincial Legislatures (Government Notice under the
Electoral Act), the nine legislatures' sizes are recalculated each
cycle. Recent (2024) sizes:

| Province | Legislature seats |
|---|---:|
| Eastern Cape | 72 |
| Free State | 30 |
| Gauteng | 80 |
| KwaZulu-Natal | 80 |
| Limpopo | 49 |
| Mpumalanga | 30 |
| Northern Cape | 30 |
| North West | 38 |
| Western Cape | 42 |

Each provincial legislature is elected within its own province as
a **single electoral district** — no sub-provincial districting.
The magnitude therefore equals the legislature size.

### 2.3 Municipal councils

#### Statutory text — Municipal Structures Act s 18 (Number of councillors)

Section 18 of the *Local Government: Municipal Structures Act 117
of 1998* delegates council-size determination to the responsible
Member of the Executive Council (MEC) of the province, subject to
a formula keyed to the number of registered voters:

> 18. (1) The MEC for local government in a province, by notice in
> the *Provincial Gazette*, must determine the number of
> councillors of each municipal council in the province in
> accordance with a formula determined by the Minister by notice in
> the *Gazette*.
>
> (2) The number of councillors determined in terms of subsection
> (1) —
>
> (a) must be a number determined in accordance with the formula
> referred to in that subsection;
>
> (b) may not be fewer than three (3) and may not exceed two
> hundred and seventy (270).

(Subsection (2)(b) sets the statutory minimum/maximum: 3 to 270
councillors. Verbatim full text and the Minister's formula notice
are a primary-source gap; description above per Schedule 1 Item 1
and the consolidated text of Act 117 of 1998.)

Recent council sizes range from approximately 7 in the smallest
local municipalities to 270 in eThekwini (Durban). Other large
metropolitan councils include the City of Johannesburg
(approximately 270), City of Cape Town (approximately 231), City
of Tshwane (approximately 214), Ekurhuleni (approximately 224),
Nelson Mandela Bay (approximately 120), and Buffalo City
(approximately 90).

#### Schedule 2 — mixed-system wards and PR seats

For municipalities falling under the *mixed system* — most
metropolitan and many local municipalities — Schedule 2 of Act 117
of 1998 provides that the council consists of:

- **An equal number of ward councillors and proportional-
  representation councillors**, with the total determined under
  s 18.

Where the total is an odd number, the schedule provides for the
half-and-half division to round to the closer integer for ward
seats; the PR component absorbs the remaining seats. The result
is that a council of e.g. 90 seats has 45 ward seats and 45 PR
seats; a council of 91 seats has 46 ward seats and 45 PR seats
(verify against Schedule 2 Item 1 — flagged as primary-source
gap).

### 2.5 Notes on the seat product

Three structural features stand out:

1. **The constitutional commitment to "proportional representation
   in general"** (Constitution ss 46(1)(d) and 105(2)(d)) is a
   floor, not a ceiling. The phrase is read in *New Nation
   Movement* and earlier Constitutional Court jurisprudence as
   permitting mixed systems (so long as the overall result is
   substantially proportional) and as foreclosing pure plurality
   systems for National Assembly and provincial legislatures. For
   U.S. drafters interested in a state-constitutional anchor for
   PR, this phrasing is a portable model: it commits the system to
   PR outcomes without prescribing a specific formula or
   architecture.

2. **The two-tier National Assembly design (200 national + 200
   regional)** is the project's working example of an integrated
   compensatory regional tier inside a single statutory framework.
   Each province's regional National Assembly seats are filled
   from a regional list submitted by the party or independent
   candidate; the national tier is allocated to produce overall
   proportionality. The mechanism is set in Schedule 1A of the
   Electoral Act (as amended 2023). This is distinct from the
   German MMP model in that there are **no single-member
   constituencies** at the National Assembly level — both tiers
   are list tiers.

3. **The municipal mixed system is the project's central case for
   ward-plus-PR architecture.** Half the council is elected by
   FPTP in single-member wards; half from PR lists, with the PR
   half allocated to produce overall proportionality. This is
   structurally similar to MMP (Germany, New Zealand) but
   implemented at the municipal rather than the national level,
   and with a single ballot rather than two votes — see
   Component 2.

### 2.6 Magnitude detail for the municipal mixed system

For the mixed-system municipalities, the effective magnitude for
the PR allocation equals the full council size (not half), because
the PR top-up is calculated against the full council and the ward
results are then deducted from each party's PR entitlement. The
following calculation operates on the face of Schedule 1 Item 5 of
Act 117 of 1998:

- Compute each party's overall seat entitlement using the total
  PR-list vote and the full council size (the **quota** is total
  valid PR votes divided by the council size, plus one).
- Subtract the number of ward seats each party has already won.
- Allocate the remaining PR seats to each party from its
  submitted PR list in list order.

The implication for the project's first analytical priority
(legibility): the magnitude is **functionally the full council
size**, but the architecture is presented to voters as a half-
ward / half-PR system. This is a drafting choice with implications
for voter understanding documented in IEC voter-education
materials.

## 3. Component 2 — Ballot Structure

### 3.1 National Assembly and provincial legislatures

#### Statutory text — Electoral Act 73 of 1998, Schedule 1A

(Schedule 1A as substituted by the Electoral Amendment Act 1 of
2023.) Each voter casts **three ballots** at a national/provincial
election:

1. A **national ballot** — for the 200 national-tier seats. Voters
   choose either a party (with its registered national list) or an
   independent candidate registered for the national tier.
2. A **regional/provincial-to-national ballot** — for the 200
   regional-tier National Assembly seats from the province in
   which the voter is registered.
3. A **provincial-legislature ballot** — for the provincial
   legislature in which the voter is registered.

Each ballot is a separate ballot paper. The voter marks one party
or one independent candidate per ballot. There is **no preference
vote** — lists are closed and binding.

(Verbatim Schedule 1A text after the 2023 amendments is a primary-
source gap. Description above per the consolidated Electoral Act
post-2023 amendment and IEC voter-education materials for the
29 May 2024 election.)

### 3.2 Municipal councils — the mixed-system ballot

#### Statutory text — Municipal Electoral Act 27 of 2000

The Municipal Electoral Act sets the operational procedures.
Voters in mixed-system municipalities cast **two ballots** for the
council:

1. A **ward ballot** — single-member constituency, FPTP. Voter
   marks one candidate. The candidate may be a party nominee or
   an independent.
2. A **PR ballot** — voter marks one party (or independent on the
   PR list, post-2021 amendments aligning local-government access
   with the *New Nation Movement* logic).

Voters in metropolitan and local-municipality areas also cast a
**district-council** ballot where the municipality is part of a
district. The three-ballot structure (ward + PR + district) is
the standard municipal ballot architecture.

For council categories without wards (small B3 municipalities and
the district-council elements), only a PR ballot is used.

#### Voter agency and the architecture

The mixed-system ballot is the project's main object of interest.
Three features:

1. **Two separate ballots, one ward / one PR.** Unlike Germany's
   two-vote MMP ballot (where both votes are tabulated together for
   personalised PR), in South Africa the ward vote determines the
   single ward winner directly, and the PR vote determines the
   overall party allocation against which ward seats are netted.
2. **The voter does not see the compensation mechanic.** A voter
   who votes for the ward candidate of Party A and the PR ballot
   for Party B is effectively expressing two different choices
   that interact through the seat-allocation calculation. The IEC
   does not publish reordering or interaction explanations on the
   ballot itself.
3. **The ward candidate may be an independent.** Independents have
   always been permitted in ward elections (s 17 of Municipal
   Electoral Act 27 of 2000 — verify Section number against the
   consolidated text; flagged). An independent who wins a ward
   takes the ward seat; the seat is **not** counted against any
   party's PR entitlement.

#### How partial winners and overhang are handled

If a party's ward wins exceed its PR entitlement (so-called
"overhang"), the council is **enlarged** by the number of overhang
seats. This is set in Schedule 1 Item 6 of Act 117 of 1998
(verify item number — flagged):

> If, in the application of the formula, a party has been allocated
> more seats in respect of a council from the wards in that council
> than the seats to which it is entitled in terms of the formula,
> the party is entitled to retain those seats and the membership
> of the council is increased accordingly.

(Verbatim text to be verified against current Schedule 1.)

The overhang treatment is the project's primary substantive
finding on the mixed-system architecture. It means:

- Wards always seat their winner.
- The PR allocation operates as a *top-up*, not as a *cap*.
- Council size can exceed the s 18 determination by overhang
  seats; the seat number stated by the MEC under s 18 is therefore
  the *nominal* council size, not the absolute upper bound.

For U.S. drafters considering an analogous design, this is
consequential. Two design choices follow:

- **South African choice**: ward seats are protected; council
  size flexes upward to absorb overhang.
- **German choice** (Bundestag, pre-2023 reform): same logic, with
  *Überhangmandate* enlarging the Bundestag.
- **German choice** (Bundestag, post-2023 reform): ward
  (constituency) seats are not protected — a constituency winner
  with the most first votes is seated only if the party's second-
  vote share supports the seat. Council size is fixed.

South Africa's municipal design tracks the older, pre-2023 German
logic. This is a portable design choice but should be made
explicitly in any U.S. statute, with the alternative (constituency-
winner exclusion) flagged for legislative decision.

### 3.3 Independent candidates after 2023

#### *New Nation Movement NPC v President of the Republic of South Africa* [2020] ZACC 11

The Constitutional Court held that the requirement of party
membership for candidacy in National Assembly and provincial-
legislature elections is inconsistent with Constitution s 19(3)(b)
(which guarantees every citizen the right "to stand for public
office and, if elected, to hold office"). The Court suspended its
declaration of invalidity for 24 months to allow Parliament to
remedy the defect.

#### Electoral Amendment Act 1 of 2023

The Act, assented to on 17 April 2023, operationalised the *New
Nation Movement* ruling. Key features:

- An **independent candidate** is a candidate who contests an
  election without being a member or nominee of a registered
  political party (Electoral Act s 1, as amended).
- Independents may contest **either the national tier (national
  ballot for National Assembly) or a single regional tier
  (regional ballot for National Assembly) or a single provincial
  legislature**, but not more than one at a time (Electoral Act
  Schedule 1A as substituted, 2023).
- Independents must satisfy signature-collection requirements (see
  Section 4.B below).
- Allocation: an independent's seat entitlement is computed using
  the same formula as for parties; the independent receives no
  seats beyond one for any single contest (since the candidate is
  a "list of one"). Any surplus votes beyond the cost of one seat
  are not transferred and are not allocated to another list.

#### *Independent Candidate Association v President* [2024] ZACC 21

In post-2024 review, the Constitutional Court considered
challenges to specific provisions of the 2023 reform, including
the asymmetry between party and independent participation in the
two tiers. (The Court's disposition is a primary-source gap I
flag; the ruling has been published on the Constitutional Court
website and SAFLII.)

### 3.5 Notes on the ballot structure

For the project's three-priority analytical lens:

- **Legibility of the law.** The closed-list architecture is the
  simplest possible ballot expression (one mark for one party or
  one independent). The voter has no intra-list agency. The PR
  half of the municipal ballot is identical in form to the
  national and provincial ballots; the ward half mirrors any
  U.S. plurality contest. Legibility for the voter is high. The
  *administrative* legibility — how the seat-allocation formula
  operates against partial winners and overhang — is lower; see
  Component 3 and IEC training materials.

- **Interconnection of statutes.** The 2023 amendment of the
  Electoral Act required parallel amendments to Schedule 1A
  (allocation), the candidate-nomination provisions (signatures,
  deposits), and the regulations promulgated by the IEC. The
  Constitutional Court's *Independent Candidate Association*
  litigation has tested how the amended statute fits with the
  Constitution's structural requirements. This is the project's
  case for **how a Constitutional Court ruling propagates through
  a layered statute** in a short legislative window.

- **Capacity for minority representation.** Closed-list PR with no
  intra-list agency places weight on the parties' internal
  candidate-selection processes. The 1996 Constitution's equality
  and redress commitments are not operationalised as a statutory
  list-composition requirement — there is no national-level
  gender quota in the Electoral Act, no ethnic-minority quota, and
  no statutorily required ordering. Parties have adopted internal
  rules (the African National Congress's 50/50 gender rule and
  ordering rules being the most discussed), but these are party-
  level commitments, not statute. See Section 5 (PROSeS) for
  outcome data.

## 4. Component 3 — Allocation Formula

### 4.1 National Assembly and provincial legislatures

#### Constitutional anchor — Schedule 3 Part A

Constitution Schedule 3 Part A sets the broad election-procedure
framework; the operative seat-allocation formula sits in
**Schedule 1A of the Electoral Act 73 of 1998** (as substituted
in 2023).

#### Statutory framework — Electoral Act Schedule 1A

The allocation operates as a **droop-style quota largest-
remainder method (Hamilton/Hare with Droop quota)**:

- The **quota** for the National Assembly is computed as: total
  valid votes (across the national ballot and the nine regional
  ballots, integrated) divided by (total seats + 1), plus 1. This
  is the Droop quota.
- Each party / independent receives one seat for each full quota
  in its total vote.
- Remaining seats are allocated by the **method of largest
  remainders** — the seats go in descending order of the surplus
  votes (the difference between the actual vote total and the
  quota threshold for the seats already won).

(Verbatim Schedule 1A text confirming the exact quota formula,
the integration of national and regional tiers, and the largest-
remainder mechanics is a primary-source gap. The description
above is consistent with: (a) IEC published seat-allocation
worked examples for the 2024 election; (b) the consolidated
Electoral Act post-2023 amendments; and (c) academic accounts of
the pre-2023 Schedule 1 / Schedule 2 mechanics, which have been
carried over with the addition of the independent-candidate
provisions.)

Provincial legislatures use the same formula applied to the
provincial vote, with the legislature's seat count as the
divisor:

- **Provincial quota** = total valid provincial votes /
  (legislature seats + 1) + 1.
- Same whole-quota + largest-remainder mechanics.

#### Ties

For the largest-remainder step, ties are decided by lot,
administered by the IEC. (Verify exact provision — flagged.)

### 4.2 Municipal councils — proportional component

#### Schedule 1 Item 5 of the Municipal Structures Act

The PR allocation in a mixed-system council operates as follows
(per Item 5 of Schedule 1 to Act 117 of 1998 — verify item
number; flagged):

1. **Quota A** = total number of valid votes cast for PR lists in
   the municipality / number of seats in the council, plus 1
   (Droop).
2. Each party's number of seats from its total PR vote = its vote
   total divided by quota A, with the integer part taken (whole
   quotas).
3. Surplus seats (the council size minus the sum of whole quotas)
   are allocated by **largest remainders** to the parties with
   the largest surpluses (the fractional remainder after taking
   the whole quota).
4. From the seat total each party is entitled to under steps 2–3,
   the party's ward seats already won are deducted.
5. The remaining PR seats are filled from each party's submitted
   PR list in order.

Where a party has won more ward seats than its overall entitlement
permits, the excess seats are retained (overhang) and the council
is enlarged.

#### Statutory text — Item 5 (excerpt, indicative wording)

(I flag the verbatim Schedule 1 text as a primary-source gap. The
key wording is broadly:)

> The quota for a seat for a council is determined by dividing
> the total number of valid votes cast in the municipality by
> the number of seats in the council, plus one. The result, plus
> one, disregarding fractions, is the quota.
>
> For each party, the number of seats is determined by dividing
> the total number of valid votes received by that party in the
> municipality by the quota for a seat, and the integer part is
> the number of seats to which the party is entitled. Surplus
> seats are awarded to parties in descending order of the
> remainder, with ties decided by the Commission by lot.

(This summary tracks the structure of the pre-2023 statute. The
2021–2023 amendments to extend access to independents at the
municipal level integrated independents into the same formula as
"lists of one" but without a list to draw replacements from on
vacancy — see Section 4.D on vacancies.)

### 4.3 No statutory percentage threshold

South Africa has **no statutory percentage threshold** at any
level. The structural threshold from magnitude alone governs.

- National Assembly: structural threshold ≈ 1/(400+1) ≈ 0.25% for
  one seat.
- Provincial legislatures: ≈ 1/(30+1) ≈ 3.2% (smallest) to 1/(80+1)
  ≈ 1.2% (largest).
- Municipal councils: varies; smallest councils (M=7) ≈ 12.5%;
  largest (M=270) ≈ 0.37%.

The absence of a statutory threshold is a deliberate constitutional
posture: the "proportional representation in general" requirement
in Constitution ss 46(1)(d) and 105(2)(d) makes any artificial
threshold a candidate for constitutional review.

### 4.4 Apparentement / list cartels

**No apparentement / list-cartel mechanism** is provided for in
any of the South African statutes. Parties may form post-election
coalitions in the relevant legislature; pre-election joint lists
or vote pooling are not statutorily available. (Verify — flagged.)

### 4.7 Notes on the allocation formula

For the project's three-priority analytical lens:

1. **Legibility.** The Schedule 1A and Schedule 1 formulas are
   procedurally drafted and walk through the steps directly. The
   eponym "Droop" is not used; the formula is set out
   computationally. This is consistent with the project's broader
   finding: working systems consistently choose procedural over
   named drafting for allocation formulas (Germany BWahlG § 5;
   Netherlands Kieswet Hoofdstuk P; Bavaria GLKrWG Art. 35).
2. **Interconnection.** The Constitutional anchor in Schedule 3
   Part A is bare; the operative rule sits in the Electoral Act's
   Schedule 1A. The Municipal Structures Act and Municipal
   Electoral Act use a structurally parallel formula at the
   municipal level — *but as a separate enactment*, not as a
   cross-reference. The split between Act 117 of 1998 and Act 27
   of 2000 means the same formula appears in two places. For U.S.
   drafters, this is an argument for **a unified electoral code
   with cross-reference** (Spain's LOREG model) over split
   statutes that re-state the same formula.
3. **Capacity for minority representation.** The Droop quota +
   largest remainders is one of the more proportionality-friendly
   formula combinations (compared to D'Hondt at low magnitude).
   Combined with the absence of a statutory threshold, this means
   small parties have arithmetic access to seats whenever
   magnitude is sufficient. At the National Assembly (M=400),
   parties with 0.25% of the national vote can win a seat — and
   in 2024 several did. At the provincial level (M=30 in smaller
   provinces), the structural threshold is higher (~3.2%). At
   small municipal councils, the structural threshold rises
   sharply with M.

#### Comparative remainder-method table

| Case | Remainder method | Effect at low M |
|---|---|---|
| South Africa (national, provincial, municipal) | Droop quota + largest remainders | Moderate small-party protection |
| Netherlands gemeenteraad (M ≥ 19) | D'Hondt | Larger-party bias |
| Netherlands gemeenteraad (M < 19) | Largest remainders with 75%-quota floor | Stronger small-party protection at low M |
| Germany federal | Sainte-Laguë / Webster | Least biased |
| Brazil federal/state/municipal | D'Hondt-style highest averages | Larger-party bias |
| Spain Congress / municipal | D'Hondt | Larger-party bias |
| Belgium municipal | Imperiali | Strong large-party bias |

South Africa is one of three project cases (along with the
Netherlands at M < 19 and the U.K.'s Welsh and Scottish regional
top-ups) that uses largest remainders with Droop quota at the
operative level. The combination produces broader effective
representation across the seat range than any of the highest-
averages alternatives.

## 5. Connected areas

### 5.1 A. Voter eligibility and registration

#### Statutory text — Electoral Act s 8 (Voters' roll)

Constitution s 46(1)(b) requires that the National Assembly is
elected on the "national common voters roll." The Electoral Act
ss 5–8 establish the common roll and the registration framework:

- South African citizens 18 years and older are eligible to vote.
- Registration is in person at the voter's voting district (the
  geographically defined area attached to a polling station) or
  online via the IEC platform.
- Permanent residents are **not** eligible to vote (in contrast
  to the Netherlands' five-year residency rule).
- South African citizens abroad may register and vote at South
  African diplomatic missions in National Assembly elections; they
  vote on the national ballot only (not regional or provincial).

The registered voters roll is closed at a date set by the IEC
ahead of each election; updates after closure require a court
order or specific statutory provision.

#### Inter-statute connection — Identification Act 68 of 1997

Voter identification operates through the **green bar-coded
identity document** or smart ID card issued under the
*Identification Act 68 of 1997*. The Electoral Act does not
duplicate identification rules; it cross-references the
identification regime in s 38 (verify section — flagged).

### 5.2 B. Candidate / list registration — *full subsection*

The most analytically central section.

#### Party registration — Electoral Commission Act 51 of 1996, s 15

Political parties register with the Electoral Commission. The
registration is one-time and remains effective until cancelled
under s 17 (verify section — flagged). The registration includes
the party name, abbreviation, distinguishing mark/logo, and a
deed of foundation; for a party to register for a national
election, it must demonstrate a minimum of 1,000 registered
voters as members; for provincial-level registration, 500; for
municipal-level registration, 100. (Verbatim provisions and the
exact thresholds are a primary-source gap; description above is
consistent with Electoral Commission Regulations under Act 51 of
1996.)

#### National Assembly and provincial-legislature candidate nomination — Electoral Act ss 26–31

For each election cycle, each party submits its national list and
its nine regional lists (for the National Assembly) and a list
for each provincial legislature it contests. The submission must
include:

- The candidate list itself, in submitter-set binding order.
- For each candidate: acceptance, the prescribed declaration
  (s 31, verify), proof of identity, and proof of South African
  citizenship.
- The prescribed deposit per list, refundable to parties that
  exceed a vote threshold per Schedule 1A (verify exact rules —
  flagged).
- For independents (since 2023): a separate nomination process —
  see below.

#### Independent-candidate nomination — Electoral Act Schedule 1A (2023)

Per the Electoral Amendment Act 1 of 2023, an independent candidate
must:

- Be a South African citizen, age 18+, eligible for election.
- Submit a nomination signed by a number of registered voters in
  the geographic area of the contest — currently set at 15% of the
  quota for one seat in the contest (per Schedule 1A item 1A as
  amended; verify exact percentage — flagged).
- Provide proof of identity, prescribed declarations, and pay the
  prescribed deposit.
- Contest only one tier — national or a single province for
  National Assembly; or one provincial legislature.

The 15% signature requirement was challenged in the *Independent
Candidate Association v President* litigation as being
disproportionate to the equivalent requirement for parties; the
Constitutional Court considered the proportionality in its 2024
ruling. (Outcome verification a primary-source gap.)

#### Municipal candidate nomination — Municipal Electoral Act ss 14–17

Per ss 14–17 of the Municipal Electoral Act 27 of 2000 (verify
section numbers — flagged):

- Each party submits a ward candidate for each ward it contests
  and a PR list for the municipality. Both can be the same person.
- Each independent candidate submits one nomination per contest
  (ward or PR list).
- Nominations require signatures of registered voters in the
  relevant area: per the regulations under the Municipal
  Electoral Act, ward candidates require approximately 50
  signatures of registered voters in that ward; PR list nominees
  require signatures scaled to the municipality. (Verify exact
  numbers and the scaling rule — flagged.)
- Each nomination form must include the candidate's identity
  document number, acceptance, and a declaration that the
  candidate is qualified.

#### Notes — independents in a list-PR framework

South Africa is the project's case for **administering independent
candidates as one-person "lists of one" inside a list-PR
framework**. Three structural choices are worth noting:

- **The independent is treated as a list for allocation
  purposes** but capped at one seat in the contest. Excess votes
  beyond the cost of one seat are surplus and do not transfer.
  This is the natural treatment when the formula is Droop quota
  with largest remainders.
- **The geographic restriction** (one tier or one province) is the
  consequential design choice. It prevents an independent with
  geographically dispersed support from aggregating votes across
  tiers. In the 2024 election, this contributed to no
  independents winning seats: the per-tier vote shares of the
  most-supported independents were below the per-tier seat cost.
- **Replacement on vacancy** is structurally different for
  independents than for parties: a party fills a vacancy from
  its next-ranked list candidate; an independent has no list, and
  the seat is filled per Schedule 1A as amended — the specific
  vacancy provisions for independents are set in s 24 of the
  Electoral Amendment Act 1 of 2023 (verify section number —
  flagged). The 2024 reform created a re-allocation rule
  whereby an independent's vacant seat reverts for filling by
  the next-best party / independent under the original
  allocation calculation (verify against statutory text).

For U.S. drafters, the South African experience produces three
concrete implementation findings:

1. The "list of one" treatment is the cleanest accommodation of
   independents within a list-PR framework. It requires only minor
   procedural drafting.
2. The signature requirement must be calibrated against the per-
   contest quota; a flat number disadvantages independents in
   high-magnitude contests and advantages them in low-magnitude
   contests.
3. The vacancy rule for independents is **not** trivial. The
   default solutions (alternates, special by-elections,
   re-allocation under the original count) each have implications
   for the original proportionality result and for the
   independent's representational claim. Pre-commit the rule in
   statute.

### 5.3 C. Campaign finance

#### Political Party Funding Act 6 of 2018

The Political Party Funding Act 6 of 2018 (in force 1 April 2021)
consolidates South African campaign finance. Key provisions:

- **Public funding** is allocated to parties represented in the
  National Assembly and provincial legislatures via the
  Represented Political Parties' Fund (s 6). Allocation has both
  an equitable (per-party flat) and a proportional (per-seat)
  component (s 6(2), verify).
- **Private donations** must be disclosed if they exceed a
  prescribed threshold (currently R200,000 per donor per year —
  verify); a single donor may not give more than R15 million per
  year (s 8, verify).
- **Donations from foreign governments and agencies** are
  prohibited (s 8).
- **Anonymous donations exceeding the disclosure threshold are
  prohibited** (s 8).
- **Independents** were brought into the Act's framework by the
  *Electoral Matters Amendment Act 14 of 2024*, which extended
  disclosure and contribution rules to independent candidates.

This is administered by the IEC. The Act does **not** cap
campaign expenditure, only donations.

For U.S. drafters: the Political Party Funding Act is a recent
(2018) and substantive primary-source case for how disclosure-
focused regulation of party finance can be drafted as a stand-
alone statute that operates in parallel with the electoral act.

### 5.4 D. Election administration / EMB

#### Constitution s 190 — the IEC as a Chapter 9 institution

Constitution Chapter 9 establishes a category of "state
institutions supporting constitutional democracy" — independent,
impartial institutions accountable only to the National Assembly.
The Electoral Commission is one of these (s 190).

> 190. (1) The Electoral Commission must —
>
> (a) manage elections of national, provincial and municipal
> legislative bodies in accordance with national legislation;
>
> (b) ensure that those elections are free and fair; and
>
> (c) declare the results of those elections within a period that
> must be prescribed by national legislation and that is as short
> as reasonably possible.

#### Electoral Commission Act 51 of 1996

The Act constitutes the IEC and its powers. The Commission has
five members (the chairperson is required to be a judge), serving
seven-year terms, appointed by the President on recommendation of
the National Assembly. The Commission may not be removed except
on prescribed grounds via a parliamentary process.

The IEC has substantial operational responsibilities:

- Maintaining the national common voters' roll.
- Conducting elections at all three levels.
- Registering parties.
- Administering candidate nominations.
- Conducting the seat-allocation calculations.
- Resolving certain election disputes at first instance.

#### Operationalised features

The IEC is the project's working example of a **single national
EMB administering all levels of government**. In contrast to the
Spanish JEC (which oversees a layered hierarchy of provincial
Juntas) or the German *Bundeswahlleiterin* (which oversees a
federal-Länder cooperative model), the South African IEC directly
administers National Assembly, provincial-legislature, and
municipal elections through its own infrastructure.

For U.S. drafters, this is a strong centralised-administration
counterpoint to the U.S. norm of state-by-state and county-level
election administration. The lesson is not portability (the U.S.
constitutional structure forecloses federal-level election
administration of state contests), but rather that
**administrative integration across levels reduces compliance
costs for parties and candidates and produces uniform
procedural standards.**

### 5.5 E. Vote count, certification, recounts, dispute resolution

#### Counting — Electoral Act ss 47–53

Counting is conducted at the voting station immediately after
the close of polls, by the IEC's voting-station staff and party
agents. The voting station's result is captured on a standard
results slip, signed by the presiding officer, and transmitted
to the IEC's results system.

The IEC's results system aggregates voting-station results to
the municipality, then the province, then nationally. The
Commission declares the result by resolution within a period
"as short as reasonably possible" under Constitution s 190(1)(c)
— typically within 5–7 days.

#### Recounts and audits

Recounts are conducted on application or on the Commission's own
motion where evidence of error exists. The procedure is in
Electoral Act s 65 (verify). The IEC also conducts results-
system audits before final declaration.

#### Dispute resolution — the Electoral Court

The **Electoral Court** is a specialised court established under
the Electoral Commission Act s 18 (verify section). It hears:

- Appeals from IEC decisions on registration, nomination, and
  candidate matters.
- Election-result challenges under the Electoral Act.

The Electoral Court is constituted from judges of the Supreme
Court of Appeal and the High Courts. Its decisions on questions
of law may be appealed to the Constitutional Court. This is one
of the strongest specialised-electoral-court systems in the
project's case set, comparable to (and structurally similar to)
Brazil's *Justiça Eleitoral*.

For U.S. drafters: a specialised electoral-disputes court is a
portable design (some U.S. states have specialised election-law
review tracks; few have dedicated courts). The South African
model demonstrates that a small, well-resourced specialised
court can dispose of election disputes within tight statutory
timetables — a quality that improves regulatory legibility for
parties and candidates.

### 5.6 F. Media access

Media access is governed by the **Independent Communications
Authority of South Africa (ICASA)** under the Electronic
Communications Act 36 of 2005 and ICASA's *Regulations on Party
Election Broadcasts and Political Advertisements* (most recently
promulgated 2024).

Key provisions:

- Public broadcasters (SABC) allocate party-election broadcast
  time on an equitable basis, with allocations to parties tied
  to representation in the previous Parliament and the number of
  candidates fielded.
- Independent candidates were brought into the framework for the
  2024 election (verify regulation citation — flagged).
- Paid political advertising is permitted on private broadcasters
  during the regulated election period, subject to disclosure
  rules under the Political Party Funding Act.

### 5.7 G. Districting law

#### Municipal Demarcation Act 27 of 1998

For municipal wards, the *Local Government: Municipal Demarcation
Act 27 of 1998* establishes the **Municipal Demarcation Board**.
The Board determines:

- Municipal boundaries (the outer boundaries of each council's
  jurisdiction).
- Internal ward boundaries within each mixed-system municipality.

The Board operates on a five-year cycle aligned with the local-
government election cycle. Ward boundaries are reviewed and
re-drawn before each election cycle.

#### Statutory criteria for ward delimitation

Per s 25 of the Demarcation Act (verify section), the Board
considers:

- The interdependence of people, communities, and economies.
- The need for cohesive, integrated, and ungroupable areas.
- Existing administrative and traditional-authority boundaries.
- Topography and accessibility.
- Equal voter numbers across wards within the municipality
  (population-equality target with a tolerance band).

The Board operates under a quasi-judicial public-consultation
regime: ward proposals are published, written objections invited,
public hearings conducted, and final determinations promulgated
in the *Provincial Gazette*.

For U.S. drafters, the Demarcation Board is a substantive
**independent-commission redistricting model**. Comparable in
structure to the Australian Electoral Commission's redistribution
process and to the U.K.'s Boundary Commission, it is one of the
project's strongest cases for **an independent body conducting
internal-district delimitation outside the partisan legislative
process**.

## 6. PROSeS performance diagnostic

The empirical anchors are the **29 May 2024 national / provincial
elections** (the most recent national cycle, and the first under
the 2023 reform) and the **1 November 2021 local-government
elections** (the most recent municipal cycle). Sources:

- IEC (Electoral Commission of South Africa) — official results,
  voter-roll data, results-portal archives.
- Statistics South Africa (Stats SA) — demographic and population
  data.
- Electoral Integrity Project (PEI) scores for South Africa
  through 2024.
- Academic literature on South African elections (notably the
  *South African Journal of Political Studies* and *Politikon*).

### 6.1 Process Design

**Public participation**: the IEC operates an extensive voter-
registration outreach programme and a public-consultation regime
for ward delimitation (Municipal Demarcation Board). The 2023
Electoral Amendment Act was preceded by parliamentary public
hearings.

**Probity and impartiality**: the IEC's Chapter 9 status, the
specialised Electoral Court, and Constitutional Court oversight
together produce a layered integrity architecture. South Africa is
consistently rated in the upper tier on the Electoral Integrity
Project's PEI index among African democracies, with declines
during the 2010s associated with disputes over voter-roll
accuracy and the IEC's leadership.

**Accountability**: the Commission reports to the National
Assembly. Specific incidents (e.g., the 2014 dispute over voter
addresses, *Kham v Electoral Commission* [2015] ZACC 37) have
produced binding judicial findings against the IEC, demonstrating
that the accountability mechanism functions.

### 6.2 Resource Investment

The IEC is funded through the national budget. Public funding
flows to parties via the Represented Political Parties' Fund
(Political Party Funding Act s 6) and constituency-allowance
funding via the National Assembly.

Voter education is funded through the IEC's budget and through
civil-society partnerships. Civil-society election-observation
funding is available through several public-funding instruments
and donor sources.

### 6.3 Service Output Quality

**Convenience**: in-person voting at the voter's registered voting
station on election day; special votes for the disabled,
hospitalised, and pregnant; overseas voting at diplomatic missions.
Voter registration available online since the 2020s; in-person
registration weekends scheduled in advance of each cycle.

**Accuracy**: paper ballots used at all levels; manual count at
the voting station; electronic capture and aggregation. Spoiled
ballot rates: approximately 1.0%–1.2% at the 2024 election (verify
against IEC published figures — flagged).

**Enforcement and efficiency**: prosecution of electoral offences
under the Electoral Act and Municipal Electoral Act is
administered through ordinary criminal justice channels; the IEC
operates a complaints and prosecutorial referral system.

### 6.4 Service Outcomes

**Voter turnout** at the 2024 National Assembly election was
**~58.6%** of registered voters (verify final figure — flagged),
the lowest since 1994. As a share of voting-age population (which
includes unregistered citizens), the figure is closer to ~42%.

**Voter registration**: approximately 27.8 million registered
voters in 2024; voting-age population approximately 41 million.
Registration completeness has been a continuing concern,
particularly among younger voters.

**Equity / minority representation** — this is the project's
central interest for South Africa.

*Gender representation*. South Africa is in the top tier of the
project's case set for women's parliamentary representation,
driven largely by the **African National Congress's voluntary
50/50 zebra-list ordering** rather than statutory quota. In the
2024 National Assembly, women hold approximately 44% of seats
(verify — flagged). The number has trended above 40% since 2009.

The statutory framework contains **no gender quota**. Parties
adopt internal rules; the ANC's 50/50 zebra-list rule and analogous
EFF commitments are the principal drivers. For U.S. drafters
this is consequential: the South African experience demonstrates
that party-internal commitments can produce substantial gender
representation **without** statutory mandate — but the result is
asymmetric across parties (the DA and IFP have historically lower
female representation than the ANC and EFF), and is volatile to
party-internal politics.

*Racial / ethnic representation*. The 1996 Constitution's
commitment to equality and redress, combined with party
candidate-selection practices that explicitly track racial
demographics, produces a National Assembly racial composition
that approximates the national population. This is not a
statutory rule but a party-level practice — and as with gender,
the asymmetry across parties is observable.

*Linguistic / cultural minorities*. South Africa has 11 official
languages, with English typically as the language of legislative
business. Minority-language candidates and parties are accommodated
through the political-party system. The IFP (predominantly
isiZulu-speaking) and the Vryheidsfront Plus (predominantly
Afrikaans-speaking) are examples of parties with strong cultural-
linguistic constituencies that consistently win representation.

*Geographic and concentration effects*. The two-tier National
Assembly structure (national list + regional lists) ensures that
regionally-concentrated parties (such as the IFP, which is
predominantly KwaZulu-Natal) win representation through the
regional tier even where their national vote share is low. The
constitutional design here parallels the per-province D'Hondt
structure of the Spanish Congress — in both cases, regional
minorities are well-accommodated by per-region allocation, but
the practical mechanism differs (Spain's per-province D'Hondt;
South Africa's two-tier integrated allocation).

*Roma / dispersed-minority parallel*. As in Spain (Roma) and
elsewhere, ethnic-religious minorities whose membership is
geographically dispersed have no analogous statutory mechanism
for representation. South African parties have not generally
adopted internal candidate-selection rules to address dispersed
minorities (e.g., Muslim, Hindu, Indian-origin) — but party-
internal practices have produced some representation of these
groups in provinces where they are concentrated (KwaZulu-Natal
for Indian-origin South Africans, Western Cape for Cape
Coloured / mixed-race communities).

#### Local-government representation outcomes

At the 2021 local-government elections, the mixed-system councils
produced overall composition close to the PR-vote shares of the
parties, demonstrating that the compensatory mechanism works as
designed in most cases. Overhang occurred in several smaller
municipalities where a single party won most or all wards but
held a smaller PR vote share — the council was enlarged
accordingly. (Specific worked examples and incidence figures for
overhang in 2021 are a primary-source gap; verify against IEC
2021 election report.)

### 6.5 Stakeholder Satisfaction

The 2024 election was the first contested under the new
Electoral Amendment Act. Stakeholder satisfaction with the new
framework is mixed: the *Independent Candidate Association v
President* litigation reflects ongoing disagreement over the
balance struck between party and independent participation.
General satisfaction with the IEC's administrative performance
remains positive in academic and observer reports, though some
declines from the post-1994 peak are documented.

## 8. Gardner portability assessment for US implementation

### 8.1 Universal-vs-particular classification

| Provision | Type | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Constitutional commitment to "proportional representation in general" (ss 46(1)(d), 105(2)(d)) | **Universal** | The phrasing commits the system to PR outcomes without prescribing formula or architecture; portable to any U.S. state-constitutional or charter context as a flexible PR commitment. |
| Two-tier integrated National Assembly (200 national + 200 regional) | **Universal** | The integrated compensatory design is portable; the specific allocation of regional seats by registered voters is a tunable parameter. |
| Municipal ward + PR mixed system (Schedule 1 / Schedule 2 of Act 117 of 1998) | **Universal** | The ward + PR top-up architecture, with overhang absorbed by council enlargement, is directly portable as a municipal design. |
| Droop quota + largest remainders | **Universal** | Standard formula combination; portable. |
| No statutory percentage threshold | **Universal** | A design choice with constitutional justification; portable. |
| Independent-candidate "list of one" treatment in list PR | **Universal** | The mechanism for accommodating independents in a list-PR framework is portable and computationally simple. |
| Single national EMB administering all levels | **Particular** | Reflects South African unitary-state structure; less directly portable to U.S. federal system. The administrative-integration lesson is more general. |
| Municipal Demarcation Board (independent ward delimitation) | **Universal** | Independent-commission redistricting; portable. |
| Specialised Electoral Court | **Universal** | Specialised electoral-disputes court; portable, though U.S. constitutional structure may channel into existing courts with electoral specialisation rather than a dedicated court. |
| Political Party Funding Act 6 of 2018 (disclosure framework) | **Hybrid** | The disclosure architecture is portable; the institutional framework (centralised IEC administration, parliamentary oversight) is South-African-particular. |
| Party-internal voluntary gender quotas | **Particular** | Reflects party-internal politics, not statute. The U.S. analog is the parties' own delegate-selection rules, which are not amenable to statutory drafting. The Spanish LO Art. 44 bis model is the more directly portable statutory alternative. |
| Overhang absorbed by council enlargement | **Universal** | The design choice (ward-seat protection at the cost of fixed council size) is portable; the alternative (constituency-winner exclusion as in post-2023 Germany) should be considered explicitly. |

### 8.2 Top three takeaways for US state/local implementation

1. **The municipal mixed system is the closest existing real-world
   analog to a U.S. ward-plus-PR design.** Half the council from
   single-member wards (FPTP) plus half from PR top-up lists,
   with the PR allocation calculated against full council size
   and ward wins netted out, is the architecture set in Schedule
   1 and Schedule 2 of the Municipal Structures Act 117 of 1998.
   The overhang treatment — wards protected, council enlarged —
   is a deliberate design choice that any U.S. drafter should
   make explicitly. The alternative (constituency-winner
   exclusion, post-2023 Germany) is the principal counter-model.
   The South African statute supplies a complete and substantive
   drafting template for the ward-plus-PR top-up.

2. **Independents can be accommodated in list PR with minor
   statutory drafting.** The "list of one" treatment in the
   Electoral Amendment Act 1 of 2023 (Schedule 1A of the
   Electoral Act, as substituted) integrates independent
   candidates into the same Droop-quota + largest-remainder
   formula used for parties. Three drafting questions must be
   pre-committed: (a) signature requirements (scaled to the
   per-contest quota or flat?); (b) geographic restrictions
   (one tier or multiple?); (c) vacancy rule (alternates,
   by-election, or re-allocation under the original count?).
   The South African statute makes specific choices on each;
   U.S. drafters can adapt or vary them, but the framework
   shows that each must be made.

3. **A constitutional commitment to "proportional representation
   in general" is a flexible and tested formulation.** The
   phrasing in Constitution ss 46(1)(d) and 105(2)(d) commits
   the framework to PR outcomes without prescribing formula,
   threshold, or architecture. For U.S. state-constitutional or
   municipal-charter drafting that seeks a flexible PR commitment
   that survives changes in implementing statute, the South
   African phrasing is a portable model. It also creates a
   constitutional basis for challenges to any statutory provision
   (such as a high threshold) that would produce non-proportional
   outcomes — a feature the *New Nation Movement* litigation
   demonstrated.
