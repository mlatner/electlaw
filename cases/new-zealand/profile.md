# New Zealand — House of Representatives (national MMP) and local government

## 0. Case metadata

- **Country**: New Zealand (Aotearoa).
- **Sub-jurisdiction**: National. The case also documents the
  local-government regime in outline; the analytical centre is
  the national House of Representatives.
- **Level of government**: National (House of Representatives,
  the single chamber of the New Zealand Parliament; the
  Legislative Council was abolished in 1951). Local government
  is covered in outline only.
- **Electoral system family**: **Mixed Member Proportional
  (MMP)** at national level since 1996. Each elector casts two
  votes: a **party vote** (used to allocate seats nationally
  under Sainte-Laguë / Webster) and an **electorate vote** (cast
  in a single-member electorate under first-past-the-post).
  Local-government councils choose between **Single Transferable
  Vote (STV)** and **first-past-the-post (FPP)** per Local
  Electoral Act 2001 s. 5; STV is treated here as administrative
  scaffolding only, not as a vote-conversion case.
- **Governing statutes**:
  - **Electoral Act 1993** (1993 No. 87), as amended — the
    principal statute for parliamentary elections. Establishes
    MMP, electorate boundaries, the Māori roll mechanism, list
    nomination, the threshold and seat-allocation procedure,
    and the entrenched-provisions regime.
    <https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/1993/0087/latest/whole.html>
  - **Constitution Act 1986** (1986 No. 114) — codifies the
    Sovereign, Executive, Legislature, and Judiciary in a single
    Act of Parliament; together with a body of "constitutional
    statutes" (the Electoral Act 1993, the New Zealand Bill of
    Rights Act 1990, the Senior Courts Act 2016, the Supreme
    Court Act 2003, the Treaty of Waitangi Act 1975, and others)
    discharges the function that a single codified constitution
    discharges elsewhere.
    <https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/1986/0114/latest/whole.html>
  - **New Zealand Bill of Rights Act 1990** (1990 No. 109),
    s. 12 — affirms the right of every New Zealand citizen of
    or over the age of 18 years to vote in genuine periodic
    elections by equal suffrage and by secret ballot, and to
    stand for election.
    <https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/1990/0109/latest/whole.html>
  - **Electoral (Māori Electoral Option) Legislation Act 2022**
    (2022 No. 50) — amended the Electoral Act 1993 to make the
    Māori Electoral Option continuously available (replacing the
    quinquennial option window) and to extend the period during
    which roll choices may be exercised closer to an election.
    <https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2022/0050/latest/whole.html>
  - **Electoral Referendum Act 1993** (1993 No. 101) —
    governing the 1993 binding referendum that adopted MMP and
    the 2011 confirming referendum.
  - **Local Electoral Act 2001** (2001 No. 35) — sets the
    statutory choice between STV and FPP for local authorities,
    Single Transferable Vote regulations, and other local-
    election procedure. Sections 5, 27–30 are the architectural
    anchors.
    <https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2001/0035/latest/whole.html>
  - **Local Electoral Regulations 2001** (SR 2001/145), as
    amended — implementing regulations setting out the STV count
    procedure (Meek's method) and other ballot detail.
  - **Electoral Regulations 1996** (SR 1996/93), as amended —
    implementing detail for parliamentary elections.
- **Constitutional anchor**: New Zealand has **no codified
  constitution**. The Constitution Act 1986 is an ordinary Act
  of Parliament that may be amended by ordinary majority. The
  Electoral Act 1993 s. 268 is the principal exception: it
  designates a small set of "reserved provisions" that may only
  be amended by a majority of all electors voting in a binding
  referendum, **or** by a 75% majority of all the members of the
  House of Representatives. This is the most analytically
  significant entrenchment provision in the project's case set.
- **EMB**: the **Electoral Commission** (*Te Kaitiaki Take Kōwhiri*),
  an independent Crown entity, established in its current form
  by Part 2 of the Electoral Act 1993 (as substituted by the
  Electoral (Administration) Amendment Act 2010 and the
  Electoral (Administration) Amendment Act 2011). The Commission
  has principal responsibility for registering parties,
  administering parliamentary elections, allocating list seats,
  declaring results, and providing public information on the
  electoral system.
- **Last regular elections**: **14 October 2023** (House of
  Representatives). The next general election is required by
  s. 17 of the Constitution Act 1986 and ss. 125–127 of the
  Electoral Act 1993 within three years of the first sitting of
  Parliament after the preceding general election; the latest
  permissible date is in 2026. Local government — **8 October
  2022** (next regular October 2025) on a three-year cycle under
  the Local Electoral Act 2001 s. 10.
- **Royal Commission on the Electoral System (1986)** —
  *Towards a Better Democracy*, the foundational policy report
  that recommended MMP. Cited for historical legibility context;
  not substantive authority.
- **Analyst**: this profile, 2026-05-15.

### Why this case is in the project

New Zealand is the project's **canonical MMP comparator**.
Three features anchor that designation:

1. **MMP at national level with a national list tier.** Since
   the 1996 general election the House of Representatives has
   been elected by MMP. The current architecture is set by the
   Electoral Act 1993 (originally enacted by Part 1 of the
   Electoral Act 1993, replacing the FPP regime of the Electoral
   Act 1956): a statutory minimum of 120 seats divided into
   single-member electorates and a national pool of list seats,
   with list seats allocated nationally using Sainte-Laguë /
   Webster to produce overall proportionality between parties'
   party-vote shares and parliamentary seat shares. Compared to
   Germany (federal: 16 *Land* lists with two-level
   distribution, BWahlG § 5) and Scotland (Holyrood: 8 regional
   sub-pools, Scotland Act 1998 s. 8), the New Zealand list tier
   is a **single national pool**, producing higher aggregate
   proportionality than either of the other principal MMP
   designs in this project.

2. **The Māori electoral option and dedicated Māori
   electorates.** Sections 35, 36, 45, and 76–79 of the
   Electoral Act 1993 establish a parallel **Māori roll** for
   electors of Māori descent who choose to be enrolled on it
   (s. 76), and direct that the **number of Māori electorates
   is determined by Māori-roll population** under the formula in
   s. 45. As of the 2023 general election there are **7 Māori
   electorates** (out of 72 single-member electorates). The
   Māori-roll mechanism, the population-driven number of Māori
   electorates, and the elector-choice device (the **Māori
   Electoral Option**) together constitute the project's most
   developed example of dedicated descriptive-representation
   seats embedded in a PR architecture. The Electoral (Māori
   Electoral Option) Legislation Act 2022 (2022 No. 50)
   converted the option from a quinquennial window to a
   continuously available roll choice. This is the project's
   reference design for a dedicated-minority-representation
   provision embedded in an MMP system.

3. **Statutory entrenchment of the core electoral provisions.**
   Section 268 of the Electoral Act 1993 designates a closed
   list of "reserved provisions" — covering the membership of
   the House of Representatives (s. 35, s. 36 of the
   Constitution Act 1986), the term of Parliament (s. 17 of the
   Constitution Act 1986), the Representation Commission, the
   division of New Zealand into general and Māori electorates,
   the allocation formula, and the voting age — and provides
   that those provisions may be amended only by a **majority of
   all electors voting** in a referendum or by **75% of all the
   members of the House** voting in favour at the third reading.
   The s. 268 mechanism is the project's clearest example of a
   statutory super-majority lock on the core electoral system
   inside an otherwise ordinary-statute regime — a design
   feature that closely parallels the Scotland Act 1998 s. 31(1A)
   protected-provisions rule but predates it and operates by
   referendum-or-supermajority rather than by supermajority
   alone.

A fourth point worth flagging up front: the Electoral Act 1993
specifies the Sainte-Laguë / Webster procedure for list-seat
allocation **algorithmically, in the body of the statute** (s.
191 and Schedule 1 — see Component 3), and pairs it with a
**5% party-vote threshold or one electorate-seat backdoor**
(s. 191(4)). The combination of an algorithmic codification of
the formula and an explicit statutory backdoor is the project's
most legible threshold-and-formula pairing.

## 1. Statutory architecture — the Electoral Act 1993 framework

The Electoral Act 1993 is organised into 12 Parts and 4
Schedules. The architecturally significant parts are:

| Part | Subject | Sections |
|---|---|---|
| **Part 1** | The Electoral Commission and other electoral officers | ss. 4–34 |
| **Part 2** | Electors, electoral rolls, electoral districts | ss. 35–113 |
| **Part 3** | Voting | ss. 114–127 |
| **Part 4** | Registration of political parties and logos | ss. 62–71G |
| **Part 5** | Nominations of constituency and list candidates | ss. 128–146 |
| **Part 6** | The poll | ss. 147–181 |
| **Part 7** | Counting and return | ss. 178–193 |
| **Part 8** | Election petitions | ss. 229–267 |
| **Part 9** | Restrictions on amendment (entrenched provisions) | s. 268 |
| **Part 10** | Election expenses and donations | ss. 204A–283G |

(Section numbers reflect the current consolidated text on
legislation.govt.nz. The Act has been amended frequently since
1993; the part-and-section structure above is the post-2010
arrangement following the Electoral (Administration) Amendment
Act 2010 and subsequent amendments.)

The architecture pairs an ordinary-statute Act with a small
referendum-or-supermajority lock on its core. **Most of the
Electoral Act 1993 can be amended by ordinary parliamentary
majority**; only the reserved provisions listed in s. 268(1)
require the special procedure of s. 268(2). For US drafters,
this is the project's clearest documented model of *partial*
statutory entrenchment: a comprehensive electoral statute that
carries a small, surgically defined entrenchment subset rather
than super-entrenching the whole Act.

### Statute-regulation-Commission cascade

Operational interpretation runs through:

1. **Constitution Act 1986 (s. 17, s. 35)** — Parliamentary
   composition and term, as backed by NZ Bill of Rights Act
   1990 s. 12 (right to vote and stand).
2. **Electoral Act 1993** — operational rules. Amendable by
   ordinary majority except for s. 268 reserved provisions.
3. **Electoral Regulations 1996** — implementing detail.
4. **Electoral Commission instructions and decisions** — formal
   administrative interpretation. Under Electoral Act 1993 s. 5
   the Commission's principal objective is "to administer the
   electoral system impartially, efficiently, effectively, and
   in a way that — (a) facilitates participation in
   parliamentary democracy; (b) promotes understanding of the
   electoral system and associated matters; (c) maintains
   confidence in the administration of the electoral system."
5. **Court of Appeal / Supreme Court** — judicial review and
   election petitions under Part 8.

For US drafters: the s. 5 statement of the Commission's
"principal objective" is a portable drafting device. It sets
purposive criteria for administrative discretion in a single
sentence, against which Commission decisions can be reviewed.

## 2. Component 1 — Seat Product (Assembly Size × District Magnitude)

### Statutory text — Electoral Act 1993 s. 35

> **s. 35 Members of Parliament**
> The House of Representatives shall consist of —
>   (a) such number of members elected by way of seats allocated
>       under section 191 (commonly referred to as list seats)
>       as is determined under that section; and
>   (b) one member for each of the General electoral districts
>       and one member for each of the Māori electoral districts
>       (commonly referred to as electorate seats).

(Section 35 wording follows the structure of the post-1996
consolidated text; section 35(1) explicitly references s. 191
as the allocation rule for list seats. Verbatim text not
retrieved at drafting time — flagged in s. 10.)

### Statutory text — Electoral Act 1993 s. 36 (size of House)

Section 36 sets the working size of the House:

- **s. 36(1)**: there are to be such number of General
  electorates and Māori electorates as is determined under s.
  45 (population formula) — currently 65 General electorates
  and 7 Māori electorates for a total of 72 electorate seats.
- **s. 36(2)** specifies the statutory minimum total membership
  of the House at **120 members**.
- The actual size of any Parliament may be greater than 120 if
  **overhang seats** are produced — i.e., if a party wins more
  electorate seats than its party-vote share would entitle it
  to under s. 191. In that case, the party retains the extra
  seats and the House size increases for that Parliament.

(The exact statutory mechanism by which overhang increases the
total is set in s. 191, the seat-allocation provision; see
Component 3.)

### Statutory text — Electoral Act 1993 s. 45 (Representation Commission population formula)

Section 45 sets the **population formula** that determines the
number of General and Māori electorates:

- **s. 45(2)**: The number of General electorates in the South
  Island is fixed by Schedule 1A at **16**. (This is the
  Otago/Canterbury/Westland baseline; the figure has been 16
  since 1996 and is the structural anchor of the formula.)
- **s. 45(6)**: The **South Island General electoral quota** is
  the South Island general-electoral-population total divided
  by 16.
- **s. 45(7)–(8)**: The number of North Island General
  electorates is the North Island general-electoral-population
  total divided by the South Island electoral quota (with
  conventional rounding). The number of Māori electorates is
  the Māori-electoral-population total divided by the same
  quota (with conventional rounding).
- **s. 45(9)**: The "Māori electoral population" used in the
  formula is computed from those electors of Māori descent who
  have **chosen to enrol on the Māori roll** at the most recent
  Māori Electoral Option, plus a population-modelled component
  for under-18s of Māori descent in households of Māori-roll
  electors.

The architectural result: **Māori roll choice directly
determines the number of Māori electorates**. As Māori electors
move from the General roll to the Māori roll, the Māori
electoral population grows and (over a threshold population
size) the number of Māori electorates increases by one. The
total electorate count is **the South Island anchor (16) + a
North Island General number ≈ population-driven + a Māori
number ≈ Māori-roll-population-driven**.

(For 2023: 16 South Island General + 49 North Island General +
7 Māori = 72 single-member electorate seats. Combined with the
target 120 total, the list pool was 48 seats.)

### Statutory text — Constitution Act 1986 s. 17 (term of Parliament)

(The reserved-provisions list in Electoral Act 1993 s. 268
includes Constitution Act 1986 s. 17 — term of Parliament:
three years.)

### Districts and magnitude

The single-member electorates use **magnitude M = 1, FPP**.
The list tier is a single national pool. Algebraically:

- **electorate tier**: M = 1, 72 districts, FPP within each.
- **list tier**: M = 48 nominally (120 minimum total − 72
  electorates), national pool, closed list per s. 127.

The list tier's effective magnitude is determined by the
combined operation of (a) the 120-seat minimum, (b) the
electorate count, and (c) any overhang seats produced.

### Notes — population formula and the Māori roll

Three architectural points are central:

1. **Population-driven Māori-electorate count.** Section 45's
   formula is the project's clearest documented example of a
   dedicated-minority-representation mechanism that **scales
   with the size of the minority's roll choice**. The number of
   Māori electorates is not statutorily fixed; it is
   re-computed at each five-yearly redistribution from the
   Māori-roll population at the most recent option. The
   mechanism's design feature is that **descriptive
   representation expands or contracts with elector choice**.

2. **The South Island anchor.** Section 45(2)'s fixing of South
   Island General electorates at 16 is the structural anchor
   that determines the General electoral quota for the whole
   country. North Island General and Māori electorate counts
   are derived from the quota implied by 16 South Island seats.
   This is a particular New Zealand drafting choice — a
   compromise reached in the 1965 amendment that gave
   geographic stability to the North/South Island split — that
   is **not directly portable** but illustrates the use of a
   fixed-anchor approach to managing inter-regional electorate
   distribution.

3. **The 120-seat minimum.** Section 36(2) sets a **floor**,
   not a ceiling. Overhang seats may produce parliaments of
   121–124 members. Compared with Germany's 630-seat fixed
   total (BWahlG § 1(1), post-2023 reform) and Scotland's 129
   fixed total (Scotland Act 1998 s. 1), the New Zealand
   architecture allows House size to vary with electoral
   outcomes. The 2008 and 2011 Parliaments had 122 members
   (overhang from the Māori Party); the 2023 Parliament has
   123 (overhang from Te Pāti Māori).

### Local government — single sentence on assembly size

Council size in territorial authorities (city, district,
regional councils) is set by the Local Government Act 2002
Schedule 7 and supplementary statutes for individual councils
(e.g., the Local Government (Auckland Council) Act 2009 for the
20-member Auckland Council). Per s. 19 of the Local Electoral
Act 2001, council sizes range from 5 to 29 members for
territorial authorities; Auckland's 20-member council is
created under its own enabling statute. Magnitude per ward
depends on each council's ward structure decided in its
representation review under s. 19I of the Local Electoral Act
2001.

## 3. Component 2 — Ballot Structure

### Statutory text — Electoral Act 1993 s. 150 (two-vote ballot)

Section 150 sets the form of the parliamentary ballot paper.
The paper carries:

- A **left column** listing all electorate candidates for the
  voter's electorate, each with name and party affiliation (or
  "Independent" if none); the voter ticks **one** candidate.
- A **right column** listing all registered parties that have
  submitted a list, each with party name and logo; the voter
  ticks **one** party.

Section 150 specifies the form, layout, party-listing order,
and the requirement that each voter cast one electorate vote
and one party vote on a single ballot paper. The two votes are
counted separately (s. 178–180).

### Statutory text — Electoral Act 1993 s. 127 (closed party list)

Section 127 governs the **list of candidates** that each
registered party may submit:

- **s. 127(1)**: a registered party may submit a list of
  candidates ordered as the party determines.
- **s. 127(3)**: the list may contain up to 65 candidates (the
  statutory maximum; in practice major-party lists fall well
  below this cap).
- **s. 127(4)**: the order of candidates on the list, as
  submitted, **is the order in which list seats are filled**
  (closed list — the voter cannot reorder).
- **s. 127(5)**: a candidate may stand both in an electorate
  and on a party list ("dual candidacy"). A successful
  electorate candidate is treated as filling an electorate
  seat and is "passed over" on the list — the next candidate
  in list order who has not been seated takes the next list
  vacancy (s. 191(6)).

The closed-list architecture mirrors Germany (BWahlG § 27) and
Scotland (Scotland Act 1998 s. 5A): the voter's party vote
endorses the party's submitted candidate order; intra-list
seat assignment runs in submitted order.

### Statutory text — Electoral Act 1993 s. 191 (allocation of list seats)

Section 191 is the operative seat-allocation provision; the
two key threshold and ballot-related sub-sections for
Component 2 are:

- **s. 191(2)**: only the party votes for parties qualifying
  under sub-section (4) are counted in the allocation.
- **s. 191(4)**: a party qualifies for the allocation of list
  seats if it has either —
  - (a) **received at least 5% of all valid party votes** in
        the election, OR
  - (b) **won at least one electorate seat**.

(Sub-section (4) is the "5%-or-one-electorate" threshold — the
"backdoor" threshold rule familiar in international comparative
PR literature.)

### Statutory text — Electoral Act 1993 s. 128 (registration of parties)

Section 128 (with ss. 62–71G in Part 4) establishes party
registration. Operational features:

- **s. 71(2)**: a party may be registered if it has at least
  500 current financial members entitled to vote.
- **s. 67**: the party name must not be obscene, must not be
  longer than 25 characters, must not so resemble another
  registered party's name as to confuse voters, and may not be
  inappropriate for other specified reasons.
- **s. 67A**: each registered party may register a party logo
  for display on the ballot.

### Ballot architecture

The parliamentary ballot is the project's canonical example of
a **single-paper two-vote ballot**. The voter sees the
electorate column and party column side by side, marks one tick
in each column, and the votes are tallied separately. The
design is materially identical to the German *Erststimme*-
*Zweitstimme* ballot (BWahlG § 30(2)) and the Scottish
Holyrood ballot (Scotland Act 1998 ss. 5–6), with one
documented difference: the Electoral Act 1993 ballot lists
**all electorate candidates with their party affiliation in
the left column**, while the German ballot prints only the
constituency candidate's party and name; this gives the New
Zealand voter cross-referenceable information about
candidate-party association on a single sheet.

The party-vote column lists **all registered parties that have
submitted a list**, with party name and logo (s. 150). Order on
the ballot is determined by lot under s. 150(3); randomization
removes any donkey-vote advantage from alphabetical or
incumbent-first listing.

### Notes — ballot architecture

Three features warrant comment for the project's purposes:

1. **The single-paper two-vote design** is the project's most
   widely deployed MMP ballot design and the most copied. The
   Electoral Act 1993 specifies this design in primary statute
   (s. 150), rather than leaving the form to the
   administering body's regulation. The drafting choice is
   portable.

2. **Independent candidates have a constituency-level path
   only.** Section 128 limits list submission to registered
   parties (Part 4). An individual cannot submit a "list of
   one" or stand on the list as a non-party candidate. This
   exactly mirrors the German federal pattern (BWahlG § 27(1):
   "Land lists may only be submitted by political parties").
   The Scottish Holyrood pattern, by contrast, permits
   individual candidates to stand on the regional list under
   Scotland Act 1998 s. 6(1); New Zealand does not. For US
   drafters considering MMP, the absence of an independent-on-
   the-list path is a deliberate New Zealand choice that
   simplifies list-tier administration at the cost of closing
   one route for independent representation.

3. **Random ballot ordering by lot** (s. 150(3)). Position on
   the ballot is set by drawing of lots, not by alphabetical
   surname or by incumbent ordering. This removes the donkey-
   vote advantage documented in alphabetical-listing
   jurisdictions (notably Scotland local-government STV under
   the Scottish Local Government Elections Order 2011 Schedule
   1 para. 21). The randomisation by lot is administratively
   simple and is one of the most directly portable Electoral
   Act 1993 drafting features.

### Local government — STV ballot

Where a council has chosen STV under Local Electoral Act 2001
s. 27, the ballot is a single-column ranked ballot. Each
voter ranks candidates by placing the numerals 1, 2, 3 in
order of preference. The vote is counted using **Meek's
method** (as specified in the Local Electoral Regulations
2001, regulation 1.59 and Schedule 1A) — a distinct STV count
algorithm that uses iterative recomputation of keep-values
rather than the WIGM surplus-transfer method used in Scotland
or the Ireland-style random-sample method. Meek's method was
selected for its mathematical properties (preservation of
preference-data fidelity) and its operational suitability for
computer counting.

STV is treated here as administrative scaffolding only. The
substantive analytical weight in this profile sits on the
parliamentary MMP regime.

## 4. Component 3 — Allocation Formula

### Statutory text — Electoral Act 1993 s. 191 (allocation of seats)

Section 191 is the operative allocation provision. The
provision applies the Sainte-Laguë / Webster procedure to
party votes, after the s. 191(4) threshold filter:

- **s. 191(1)**: After the official count, the Chief Electoral
  Officer (now the Electoral Commission) determines the
  allocation of seats to registered parties under this section.
- **s. 191(2)**: The total seats allocated to each qualifying
  party are determined using the **Sainte-Laguë formula** as
  set out in **Schedule 1A** (originally Schedule 1; the
  Schedule number has shifted with successive amendments).
- **s. 191(3)**: The list seats allocated to a party are equal
  to the party's total seat entitlement under sub-section (2)
  minus the number of electorate seats the party has won.
- **s. 191(4)**: A party qualifies for allocation of list seats
  only if —
  - (a) the party has received at least **5%** of all valid
        party votes cast in the election; OR
  - (b) the party has won at least **one electorate seat**.
- **s. 191(5)**: If a party has won more electorate seats than
  its total seat entitlement under sub-section (2), the party
  retains the electorate seats (no seat is taken from the
  electorate winner); the size of the House is increased by
  the number of overhang seats so produced.

(Sub-section (5) is the "overhang" rule. The 2023 Parliament's
123 total seats — 120 + 3 — reflects three overhang seats from
Te Pāti Māori winning more electorate seats than its party-
vote share entitled it to.)

### Statutory text — Electoral Act 1993 Schedule 1A (Sainte-Laguë procedure)

Schedule 1A sets the Sainte-Laguë / Webster procedure
algorithmically. The procedure is:

1. For each qualifying party, compute the sequence of
   **quotients**:
   - Q₁ = (party votes) / 1
   - Q₂ = (party votes) / 3
   - Q₃ = (party votes) / 5
   - Qₙ = (party votes) / (2n − 1)
2. Rank all quotients across all qualifying parties from
   highest to lowest.
3. Allocate seats in rank order until **120** (or the relevant
   total House size) is reached. Each seat is allocated to the
   party whose quotient is highest at that rank.

Per the New Zealand statutory form, the divisor sequence is
**(1, 3, 5, 7, …)** — the "standard" Sainte-Laguë / Webster
divisors. The Schedule does not use the eponym; the procedure
is set out arithmetically. The Schedule pairs with s. 191(2)
which directs the user to "the formula in Schedule 1A."

(Distinction worth noting: Denmark, Norway, and Sweden use a
**modified** Sainte-Laguë with a first divisor of 1.4 to
slightly disadvantage smaller parties. New Zealand uses **pure
Sainte-Laguë** with first divisor 1. This is the unmodified
Webster/Sainte-Laguë method.)

### Statutory text — Electoral Act 1993 s. 193 (effect of party-vote threshold)

Section 193 is the technical companion to s. 191(4): once a
party fails to qualify under s. 191(4) (no 5%, no electorate
seat), its party votes are **excluded** from the allocation
calculation entirely; the Sainte-Laguë procedure under
Schedule 1A is run on qualifying parties' votes only. This is
the operative mechanism of the threshold: sub-threshold
parties' votes are excluded, and seats are reallocated among
qualifying parties.

### Notes — formula and threshold operation

Three features are central:

1. **Algorithmic codification in primary statute.** Schedule
   1A's specification of the Sainte-Laguë procedure
   arithmetically — divisor sequence (1, 3, 5, 7, …), quotient
   ranking, seat allocation in rank order — is more direct
   than Germany's BWahlG § 5 (divisor-iteration procedure
   without divisor specification) or Scotland's Scotland Act
   1998 s. 8 (modified D'Hondt without eponym). The New
   Zealand statutory form is the project's clearest example of
   **the formula as algorithm in primary text**. A US drafter
   adopting Sainte-Laguë can lift the Schedule 1A pattern
   directly: the divisor sequence is enumerated, the ranking
   procedure is enumerated, the allocation rule is enumerated.

2. **5% + 1 electorate as the threshold design.** The
   combination of a percentage threshold with a "one-electorate
   backdoor" produces a documented effect: a small party with
   geographically concentrated support can clear the backdoor
   even below the percentage threshold. The 1996, 1999, 2002,
   2005, and 2008 Parliaments each included at least one party
   below 5% party vote but holding at least one electorate
   seat (ACT, United Future, Progressive, the Māori Party in
   multiple cycles). The threshold's interaction with the
   electorate-tier FPP produces the documented "tactical-
   voting" effect: voters in an electorate where a small party
   has a chance of winning the electorate seat may cast their
   electorate vote tactically to ensure the backdoor is
   activated. For US drafters, this is the canonical example
   of a designed-in threshold backdoor and its consequences.

3. **No apparentement.** Unlike Denmark (Folketingsvalgloven
   §§ 87–90, *listeforbund*), the Netherlands (Kieswet
   abolished apparentement in 2017), or Belgium, New Zealand
   has no statutory mechanism for parties to pool votes before
   the allocation. Each party's qualification under s. 191(4)
   is determined on its own party-vote total. The absence of
   apparentement reflects the New Zealand drafters' design
   choice that the threshold backdoor (one electorate seat)
   would supply the necessary flexibility for small parties;
   the comparative record suggests this is partially
   successful — small parties have qualified in every MMP
   election since 1996 — but the burden falls on FPP
   electorate organising rather than on inter-party
   coordination.

### Worked example — 2023 general election

Final 2023 results, as declared by the Electoral Commission
(certified 3 November 2023):

| Party | Party vote % | Electorate seats | List seats | Total seats | Status |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| National | 38.06 | 35 | 13 | 48 | over 5% |
| Labour | 26.91 | 17 | 17 | 34 | over 5% |
| Green | 11.61 | 3 | 12 | 15 | over 5% |
| ACT | 8.64 | 2 | 9 | 11 | over 5% |
| NZ First | 6.08 | 0 | 8 | 8 | over 5% |
| Te Pāti Māori | 3.08 | 6 | 0 | 6 | under 5% but won 6 electorate seats → 3 overhang seats |
| Sub-threshold (TOP, Democracy NZ, etc.) | ~5.6 combined | 0 | 0 | 0 | excluded under s. 191(4) |

Total House: **123** (120 + 3 overhang from Te Pāti Māori).

Three operational signatures of the regime are visible in the
2023 result:

1. **Threshold backdoor in operation.** Te Pāti Māori won 6
   electorate seats with 3.08% of the party vote — well below
   the 5% percentage threshold but qualifying under
   s. 191(4)(b). Without the one-electorate backdoor, Te Pāti
   Māori would have been excluded from list-seat allocation
   entirely; with it, the party retains all 6 electorate seats
   (its full seat entitlement under sub-section (2)) plus 0
   list seats. The overhang rule (s. 191(5)) protects the
   electorate-seat outcome.

2. **Sub-threshold parties excluded.** Parties below 5% that
   did not win an electorate seat (notably TOP and Democracy
   NZ) were excluded under s. 191(4); their roughly 5.6%
   combined party vote was wasted, increasing the proportional
   advantage of qualifying parties.

3. **Pure Sainte-Laguë in operation.** The Schedule 1A
   procedure, run on the qualifying parties' votes, produced
   the seat allocation. National's 38.06% / Labour's 26.91% /
   Green's 11.61% / ACT's 8.64% / NZ First's 6.08% sum to
   91.30%; the sixth qualifying party (Te Pāti Māori at 3.08%,
   qualifying by electorate backdoor) brought the total to
   94.38%. The remaining 5.62% (sub-threshold parties' votes)
   was reallocated by the Sainte-Laguë procedure to the
   qualifying parties in proportion to their quotients.

For US drafters: the 2023 result illustrates the threshold-
backdoor mechanism in operational form. A party with sharp
geographic concentration of support (Te Pāti Māori in the 7
Māori electorates) can clear the backdoor and retain
representation, while a party with diffuse support below 5%
(TOP) is excluded.

## 5. The Māori electoral option and dedicated Māori electorates

This section is the project's most developed treatment of a
dedicated descriptive-representation provision embedded in a
PR system. The provisions sit in Part 2 of the Electoral Act
1993 and Part 3 of Schedule 1.

### Statutory text — Electoral Act 1993 s. 76 (Māori roll)

Section 76 establishes the parallel **Māori electoral roll**.
Operative features:

- **s. 76(1)**: An elector of Māori descent may choose to be
  registered as an elector either of —
  - (a) a Māori electoral district, or
  - (b) a General electoral district.
- **s. 76(2)**: The choice is made by the elector, on
  enrolment or by exercising the Māori Electoral Option.
- **s. 76(3)** (post-2022 amendment): the choice may be
  changed at any time outside the three-month period
  immediately before a general election.

Pre-2022 the option was exercisable only in a quinquennial
window after each Māori electoral option period. The
Electoral (Māori Electoral Option) Legislation Act 2022
(2022 No. 50) made the choice continuously available, subject
only to the pre-election freeze in s. 76(3).

### Statutory text — Electoral Act 1993 s. 77 (descent test)

Section 77 sets the eligibility test. An elector qualifies for
the Māori roll if the elector is **of Māori descent**.
Operationalisation:

- Self-identification by the elector on the enrolment form
  (the Electoral Commission does not adjudicate descent in
  ordinary cases).
- No quantum or blood-quantum test (in contrast to some US
  tribal enrolment regimes).
- An elector may not be enrolled on both rolls simultaneously
  (s. 76 enforces single-roll enrolment).

The descent test is **self-declared**; this is the central
administrative drafting choice. The legitimacy of the system
rests on Māori self-identification, supported by the customary
genealogical structure of Māori iwi (tribal) and hapū (sub-
tribal) membership. The Electoral Commission's role is to
record the elector's choice; the Commission does not police
descent claims.

### Statutory text — Electoral Act 1993 s. 45 (Māori-roll-population formula)

(See Component 1 for the full statement of s. 45.) The
operative point for this section: the **number of Māori
electorates is determined by the Māori electoral population**
under the formula in s. 45(7)–(9). Movement of electors from
the General roll to the Māori roll increases the Māori
electoral population and, above the threshold for a new seat,
increases the number of Māori electorates by one.

### Statutory text — Electoral Act 1993 s. 35 (Māori electorates)

(See Component 1.) Section 35 fixes the number of single-
member electorate seats (both General and Māori); per the
post-2002 architecture, **Māori electorate seats are
substantively equivalent to General electorate seats**: each
returns one MP on FPP; each is contested on the Māori roll
(or General roll, respectively); each contributes to the
party's electorate-seat count under s. 191(2).

### The Māori Electoral Option (MEO)

The **Māori Electoral Option** is the statutory mechanism by
which an elector of Māori descent makes the s. 76 roll choice.
Operative features:

- **Continuous availability** (post-Electoral (Māori Electoral
  Option) Legislation Act 2022): an elector may change their
  roll at any time except during the pre-election freeze
  (s. 76(3), as amended).
- **No new opportunity required.** Pre-2022 the option was
  available only in defined option periods (typically every
  five years after each quinquennial Census). Post-2022 the
  option is continuously available — an elector who turns 18,
  or who comes of Māori descent awareness, or who simply
  changes their preference, may switch rolls at any time
  except the pre-election freeze period.
- **Pre-election freeze**: from the issuance of the writ for a
  general election until the close of polling, the rolls are
  closed for roll-switching (s. 76(3)). This stabilises the
  Māori electoral population for the purpose of computing seat
  allocations and managing electorate boundaries.

### Operational mechanics — administering the dedicated seats

Three administrative features distinguish the Māori electorate
regime:

1. **Geographic overlap.** The 7 Māori electorates **cover the
   whole country**, each electorate being defined by a
   geographic region containing a roughly equal Māori
   electoral population. A Māori-roll elector in (say)
   Auckland votes in *Tāmaki Makaurau* (the Māori electorate
   covering greater Auckland) regardless of which General
   electorate would cover their physical residence. The Māori
   electorate map is statutorily independent of the General
   electorate map.

2. **Boundary review by Representation Commission.** The
   Representation Commission (Electoral Act 1993 s. 28)
   reviews both General and Māori electorate boundaries every
   five years after the Census, applying the s. 45 formula.
   Māori-roll elector representation on the Commission is
   provided for by statute: the Commission includes a
   Government Statistician, the Chief Electoral Officer (now
   the Electoral Commission's Chief Executive), the
   Surveyor-General, two government appointees, two opposition
   appointees, and **two members representing Māori interests**
   when the Māori electorates are being considered (s. 28(2)–
   (3)).

3. **Single ballot, separate electorate competition.** A
   Māori-roll elector receives a ballot listing electorate
   candidates standing in **the relevant Māori electorate**
   only; the same elector's party-vote column lists all
   registered parties (the party-vote ballot is not split by
   roll). Roll choice therefore affects which electorate the
   elector votes in (and hence the candidate options at
   electorate level) but does not affect the elector's
   party-vote choices.

### Notes — Māori electorate regime

Three points for the project's purposes:

1. **The Māori electorate seats predate MMP.** The four
   original Māori electorates were created by the Maori
   Representation Act 1867 — initially as a temporary measure,
   continued by successive amendments, and given continuing
   statutory status by the Electoral Act 1956 and now the
   Electoral Act 1993. The 1986 Royal Commission considered
   abolishing the seats in light of MMP (which itself was
   expected to improve Māori representation through list
   selections) but recommended retention given the cultural
   and constitutional significance of the seats to Māori
   electors. The 1993 Electoral Act retained the seats and
   moved them onto the population formula at s. 45 (replacing
   the historic statutory fixed count of 4). The post-1993
   number is population-driven and has ranged from 5 (1996)
   to 7 (2002–present).

2. **Dual-track minority representation under MMP.** Māori
   representation in Parliament currently flows through three
   tracks: (i) Māori electorate seats (7 dedicated seats); (ii)
   General electorate seats in electorates with significant
   Māori populations (Māori candidates standing on the General
   roll); (iii) party-list seats (Māori candidates placed on
   major-party lists). The 2023 Parliament has 33 MPs of Māori
   descent across all three tracks. Dedicated seats establish
   a floor while the broader PR architecture allows additional
   Māori representation through party selection.

3. **Self-declared descent and the absence of a quantum
   test.** The s. 77 self-declaration mechanism is the
   project's clearest documented example of a descent-based
   dedicated-seat regime that **avoids a state-administered
   quantum test**. The administrative simplicity is
   substantial: the Electoral Commission collects roll-choice
   data, not descent evidence. For US contexts considering
   dedicated descriptive seats — most notably tribal
   representation or Black/Latino dedicated seats — the New
   Zealand model is portable in concept (self-declaration
   plus statutory roll), but the universal-vs-particular
   classification under Gardner is mixed: the universal
   element is the self-declaration design; the particular
   element is the underlying constitutional acknowledgment of
   the Treaty of Waitangi (Treaty of Waitangi Act 1975) as
   the basis for dedicated Māori representation.

### Treaty-of-Waitangi constitutional context

The Treaty of Waitangi (1840) is the foundational compact
between the Crown and Māori. The Treaty itself is not directly
enforceable in New Zealand courts (subject to limited
exceptions where statute incorporates Treaty principles), but
the **Treaty of Waitangi Act 1975** established the Waitangi
Tribunal as a forum for adjudicating Crown breaches of the
Treaty's principles, and an extensive jurisprudence on "the
principles of the Treaty" has developed since the 1980s. The
Māori electorate seats are widely understood as a partial
recognition of Treaty obligations regarding Māori political
participation; the seats are not themselves grounded in the
Treaty but are part of the broader settlement of the
Crown-Māori relationship.

For US drafters: the New Zealand Treaty framework is **not
directly portable** to the US constitutional setting, but the
underlying drafting move — establishing a statutory roll
choice with self-declaration as the eligibility test, paired
with a population-driven number of dedicated seats — is
portable as a design pattern independent of its specific
constitutional foundation.

## 6. Connected areas (brief)

### A. Voter eligibility and registration

#### Eligibility — Electoral Act 1993 s. 74 and NZ Bill of Rights Act 1990 s. 12

Per NZ Bill of Rights Act 1990 s. 12, every New Zealand
citizen of or over the age of 18 years has the right to vote
in genuine periodic elections and to stand as a candidate.
Electoral Act 1993 s. 74 elaborates:

- **Citizenship**: New Zealand citizens; or permanent
  residents who have **resided continuously in New Zealand for
  at least one year at some time** and currently reside in NZ.
- **Age**: 18 years or over on polling day.
- **Disqualifications** (s. 80): persons detained under the
  Mental Health (Compulsory Assessment and Treatment) Act 1992
  are disqualified; persons serving a prison sentence of three
  years or more are disqualified (the prisoner-disqualification
  rule was tightened to all sentenced prisoners in 2010, then
  partly relaxed in 2020 to apply only to sentences of three
  years or more; legislative reform on this issue is ongoing).

#### Registration

Voter registration is administered by the **Electoral
Commission** under Part 2 of the Act. Registration is
**near-universal and active**: the Commission proactively
contacts adults via address-linked records and runs an annual
update campaign. As of 2023, registration is approximately
**94% of the eligible population**.

The Commission maintains both the **General electoral roll**
and the **Māori electoral roll** (see Section 5).

#### Election-day enrolment

Per Electoral Act 1993 s. 86, an unregistered eligible elector
**may enrol on election day** at any polling place and cast a
"special declaration vote" which is counted after the
elector's enrolment is verified. This is the project's
documented example of **same-day voter registration** —
operating since 1993 and routinely used by approximately 3–5%
of voters at each general election.

For US drafters: New Zealand same-day registration plus
near-universal automatic update is the project's clearest
documented case of how an electoral roll can be administered
without the registration-as-barrier dynamic familiar in many
US jurisdictions.

### B. Candidate / list registration — *full subsection*

#### Statutory text — Electoral Act 1993 s. 71 (party registration)

Party registration is governed by Part 4 of the Act. Key
provisions:

- **s. 67**: party name and logo registration with the
  Electoral Commission.
- **s. 71(1)**: a party is eligible for registration if it has
  at least **500 current financial members** who are eligible
  to vote at parliamentary elections.
- **s. 71(2)**: party must have a written constitution
  containing specified democratic-internal-governance
  provisions (candidate selection by party members or
  delegates; specified party-officer accountability).
- **s. 71A**: filing of party financial statements and member
  numbers annually.

#### Statutory text — Electoral Act 1993 s. 127 (party lists)

(Quoted in Component 2.) Registered parties may submit a list
of up to 65 candidates (s. 127(3)). The list order is set by
the party; the voter cannot reorder (closed list).

#### Statutory text — Electoral Act 1993 s. 128 (electorate candidates)

Electorate nominations require:

- A statutory declaration of candidate eligibility (s. 128(1));
- A **$300 deposit** (s. 144), refundable if the candidate
  receives at least 5% of valid votes in the electorate;
- **Nomination by 2 electors** of the electorate (compare
  Scotland's 1 proposer + 1 seconder + 8 assenters, or
  Germany's 200-elector signatures for new-party constituency
  nominations);
- Party affiliation (if any) as registered under s. 67;
- "Independent" or no description (s. 130).

#### Statutory text — Electoral Act 1993 s. 144 (deposit)

The candidate deposit is **$300 per electorate candidate** and
**$1,000 per registered party list submission**. Deposits are
refundable on the percentage-of-vote threshold specified for
each office.

#### Notes — candidate registration

Three architectural features deserve emphasis:

1. **Low signature threshold for electorate candidacy.** Two
   electors suffice to nominate (s. 128). This is materially
   lower than Germany (200 signatures), Scotland (10 total
   signatories), or Spain (varying by population). The
   drafting choice prioritises ballot access at electorate
   level over the gatekeeping function of nomination
   requirements. New Zealand pairs this with a financial
   deposit (NZ$300) that operates as the primary frivolous-
   candidacy filter.

2. **Party registration thresholds.** Party registration
   under s. 71(1) requires 500 financial members. This is
   relatively low by comparative standards (the German
   threshold for "established party" status is continuous
   parliamentary representation of 5 members; Spain has no
   national membership minimum). The 500-member threshold
   permits new parties to enter the list-eligible space
   without major organisational scale, supporting the
   threshold-backdoor design at s. 191(4)(b).

3. **Democratic internal selection requirement** (s. 71(2)).
   The statutory requirement that party constitutions provide
   for member-or-delegate candidate selection is the project's
   clearest documented example of statute intervening in
   party-internal governance for democratic-internal purposes.
   Germany requires a delegates' or members' assembly under
   BWahlG § 21; New Zealand goes further in tying party
   registration itself to the existence of a statutorily-
   conforming party constitution. The provision is permissive
   in detail (parties retain control of their internal rules)
   but mandatory in form.

### C. Campaign finance

Election expenses and donations are governed by Part 6A of the
Electoral Act 1993 (ss. 204A–283G) and the Electoral
Regulations 1996. Key provisions:

- **Party expense cap** (s. 206C): a registered party's
  election expenses are capped during the regulated period
  (90 days before polling) at a sum specified per cycle. For
  2023: $1,402,000 base + $32,300 per electorate the party
  contests (Electoral Commission published figures).
- **Candidate expense cap** (s. 205C): $30,800 per electorate
  candidate (2023).
- **Disclosure thresholds** (s. 210): donations to a party
  exceeding **$30,000** are disclosed within 10 working days;
  donations exceeding **$5,000** in aggregate from a single
  donor in a year are disclosed in the annual party return.
- **Foreign donations cap** (s. 207I): foreign donations
  capped at $50 per donor per year (2020 amendment lowered
  this from $1,500).
- **Anonymous donations cap** (s. 207H): anonymous donations
  above $1,500 must be forwarded to the Electoral Commission.
- **State funding of parties**: New Zealand has **no
  systematic state-funding scheme** for political parties.
  Parties operate on member dues, donations, and (for
  parliamentary parties) parliamentary support funding for
  policy and research (Parliamentary Service Commission
  allocations).

For US drafters: the New Zealand campaign-finance regime is a
"low-state-funding, moderate-disclosure" model. The
disclosure thresholds ($30,000 immediate, $5,000 annual) are
materially higher than Germany's (€35,000 immediate, €10,000
annual; donations less than €1,000 generally not reportable);
the regulated period (90 days) is shorter than the German
year-round monitoring; foreign-donor rules are tighter (NZ$50
per donor vs. €1,000 in Germany).

### D. Election administration / EMB

The **Electoral Commission** (*Te Kaitiaki Take Kōwhiri*) is
the principal EMB. Established in its current form by the
Electoral (Administration) Amendment Act 2010 (which
consolidated the former Chief Electoral Office, Electoral
Enrolment Centre, and pre-existing Electoral Commission into
a single body), the Commission has principal responsibility
for:

- Registering parties and party logos (ss. 67–71G);
- Maintaining the General and Māori electoral rolls (Part 2);
- Administering parliamentary elections (Part 6);
- Allocating list seats under s. 191;
- Receiving and adjudicating election returns and donation
  disclosures;
- Public education on the electoral system.

Statutory governance:

- **Independence**: the Commission is established as an
  **Independent Crown Entity** under the Crown Entities Act
  2004 — placing it at the highest level of operational
  independence available to a Crown body under New Zealand
  statute.
- **Principal objective** (s. 5): "to administer the electoral
  system impartially, efficiently, effectively, and in a way
  that — (a) facilitates participation in parliamentary
  democracy; (b) promotes understanding of the electoral
  system and associated matters; (c) maintains confidence in
  the administration of the electoral system."
- **Membership**: three members appointed by the Governor-
  General on the recommendation of the House of
  Representatives (s. 4D).

#### Local-level administration

At each election, **Returning Officers** are appointed for
each electorate (s. 13) to administer voting and conduct the
electorate-level count. Returning Officers report to the
Electoral Commission.

For US drafters: the consolidated single-EMB structure
contrasts with the US federal-state-county distributed model.
The New Zealand model concentrates registration, party
administration, ballot design, results administration, and
public education in a single Independent Crown Entity; the
US distributed model produces variation across the 50 states
and ~3,000 counties. The New Zealand consolidation is the
project's documented working example of "one body, one
electoral system, one set of rules."

### E. Vote count, certification, recounts, dispute resolution

#### Count

- **Polling-place count** (s. 178): conducted at each polling
  place immediately after the close of polling. Both votes
  (electorate and party) are counted.
- **Electorate-level aggregation** (s. 179): the Returning
  Officer aggregates polling-place returns into the
  electorate total.
- **National aggregation and list allocation** (s. 191):
  conducted by the Electoral Commission once all electorate
  totals are available (typically 2–3 weeks after polling,
  after special declaration votes are processed).

#### Special declaration votes

Per s. 86, an elector who cannot vote at their assigned
polling place — or who enrols on election day — may cast a
**special declaration vote**. These votes are counted
separately and added to electorate and national totals during
the post-polling aggregation. In 2023, approximately 600,000
special declaration votes were cast (out of approximately 2.9
million total votes), reflecting the high mobility of New
Zealand electors and the same-day enrolment option.

#### Recounts

A candidate may apply for a **judicial recount** within 3
working days of the official declaration if the margin is
50 votes or fewer (s. 180). The recount is supervised by a
District Court Judge.

#### Election petitions

Per Part 8 (ss. 229–267), an **election petition** may be
filed in the High Court within 28 days of the official
declaration. Grounds include corrupt or illegal practices, or
other irregularity affecting the result. The High Court has
power to declare the election void or to declare another
candidate elected.

For US drafters: the High Court (rather than the legislature
or a specialised electoral court) hears election petitions.
This is the **judicial-route** dispute resolution model,
contrasting with Germany's *Wahlprüfungsverfahren* (Bundestag
first instance) and with Brazil's specialised *Justiça
Eleitoral*. The New Zealand judicial model is the project's
clearest documented case of dispute resolution within the
ordinary court system, without a specialised electoral court.

### F. Media access

Governed by the **Broadcasting Act 1989** (1989 No. 25), Part
6, and Electoral Commission guidance. Key provisions:

- **Free broadcasting time allocations**: registered parties
  receive free TV and radio time during the regulated period,
  allocated according to a statutory formula weighting prior
  electoral support and parliamentary representation.
- **Paid election advertising**: registered parties and
  candidates may purchase additional advertising subject to
  Electoral Act 1993 spending caps.
- **Election-day broadcasting silence**: from 7am to 7pm on
  polling day, election-related broadcasting and online
  campaigning is prohibited (s. 197).

### G. Districting law

Captured under Component 1. The **Representation Commission**
(Electoral Act 1993 s. 28) is the statutory boundary-drawing
body. It reviews electorate boundaries every five years after
each Census, applying the s. 45 population formula. The
Commission is required to draw electorates within ±5% of the
General electoral quota (s. 35(3)), and applies the same
tolerance to Māori electorates.

The Commission's recommendations are **final and binding** —
they take effect by Gazette notice without parliamentary
approval. This is the project's clearest documented case of
**judicially independent boundary review** with no
legislative override.

## 7. PROSeS performance diagnostic

The empirical anchors are the **14 October 2023 general
election** and Electoral Commission post-election reporting.
Sources include the Electoral Commission's *Report of the
Electoral Commission on the 2023 General Election* (December
2024), the Justice Select Committee's *Inquiry into the 2023
General Election* report, and academic literature on New
Zealand MMP (notably Vowles et al.'s New Zealand Election
Study series and Geddis on Electoral Law).

### Process Design

**Public participation**: the Electoral Commission runs
extensive public consultation on procedural matters. Major
reform processes — the 2012 Electoral Commission Review of
MMP, the 2022 Independent Electoral Review — operate as
statutory inquiries with public submissions.

**Probity**: no significant integrity concerns in recent
cycles. The Electoral Commission's independence (Independent
Crown Entity status) and the Auditor-General's annual review
of Commission financial statements provide standing oversight.

**Accountability**: election petitions through the High Court
under Part 8 (rare; the 2008, 2014, 2017, 2020, and 2023
elections produced no successful petitions). The Justice
Select Committee's inquiry-after-each-election process
produces a public report with recommendations.

### Resource Investment

**Sustainability**: the Electoral Commission is funded through
annual parliamentary appropriations. Operating budget for the
2023/24 financial year was approximately NZ$92 million across
the Commission's full responsibilities. The three-year
electoral cycle (Constitution Act 1986 s. 17) produces
predictable budgeting.

**Transparency**: party financial statements and donation
disclosures are published annually by the Commission;
campaign-period expense returns are filed within 70 working
days of polling and published.

**Legitimacy**: regulatory stability is high. The Electoral
Act 1993 has been substantively unchanged on the core
elements since 1996; amendments since then have refined
specific elements (Māori Electoral Option in 2022; prisoner
voting; Independent Crown Entity structure in 2010–2011) but
have not altered the MMP architecture or the 5%-or-one-
electorate threshold. The s. 268 entrenchment regime is the
documented structural reason for this stability.

**Contingency**: the 2020 election was held during the
COVID-19 pandemic; the Commission and Government acted
quickly to amend the Epidemic Preparedness Act and Electoral
Regulations to facilitate distance voting, mobile booths, and
extended pre-polling. The 2023 election operated under
normal conditions.

### Service Output Quality

**Convenience**: pre-polling has been extensively expanded;
in 2023 approximately **70% of all votes were cast before
polling day**, primarily through advance voting places open
in the 12 days before the election. Postal voting is
available on application; voter ID is not required.

**Accuracy**: invalid-vote rates at 2023 were approximately
**0.4% (electorate) and 0.2% (party)**, comparable to German
levels and lower than Scottish levels. The randomised ballot
ordering (s. 150(3)) and clear two-column ballot layout
contribute to low invalidation.

**Enforcement**: the Electoral Commission's regulatory
function is comprehensive; non-compliance findings are
published and referred to the Police where warranted.

**Efficiency**: the consolidated single-EMB structure (post-
2010) reduces duplication that characterised the pre-2010
arrangement (Chief Electoral Office + Electoral Enrolment
Centre + Electoral Commission). Final results are typically
declared within 3 weeks of polling.

### Service Outcomes

**Turnout** at the 14 October 2023 election was **78.2% of
those enrolled** (or approximately 72% of the eligible
population). This was a modest decrease from 2020 (82.2%
enrolled-turnout, the highest in three decades) but well
above the 2017 figure (79.8%) and the post-MMP-pre-2017
trend.

**Register completeness**: as noted, approximately 94% of the
eligible population is enrolled. Same-day enrolment under
s. 86 closes most of the remaining gap on election day.

**Invalid-vote rates**: 0.4% (electorate) and 0.2% (party) in
2023.

**Representation outcomes** for the 2023 Parliament:

- **Gender**: 50 women in the 123-member House (40.7%). This
  is a modest decrease from the 2020 Parliament (49.6%), but
  consistent with the post-2002 trend of 35–50% women MPs.
  The Greens and Labour list selections operate gender-
  balanced or zipping rules; National and ACT do not.
- **Māori**: 33 MPs of Māori descent (26.8%). Approximately
  17% of the New Zealand population identifies as Māori
  (2018 Census). The 26.8% Māori MP share substantially
  exceeds population share, reflecting the combined effect
  of (a) 7 dedicated Māori electorate seats, (b) Māori
  candidates winning General electorate seats, and (c) Māori
  candidates placed on major-party lists.
- **Pacific peoples**: 11 MPs (8.9%); approximately 8% of
  population identifies as Pacific.
- **Asian**: 11 MPs (8.9%); approximately 15% of population
  identifies as Asian.

#### Outcomes for the project's three priorities

1. **Legibility of the law**:
   - The Electoral Act 1993 is a single comprehensive Act;
     the major architectural choices (assembly size, MMP
     two-vote design, Sainte-Laguë formula, 5%-or-one-
     electorate threshold, Māori roll mechanism, s. 268
     entrenchment) are all locatable in identifiable
     sections.
   - Schedule 1A's algorithmic codification of the Sainte-
     Laguë procedure is unusually clean. A practitioner can
     read the divisor sequence and the seat-allocation rule
     directly from the Schedule.
   - The s. 268 entrenchment provision is the project's
     clearest documented surgical-entrenchment instrument
     — listing in one place exactly which provisions are
     "protected" and exactly which procedure applies.

2. **Interconnection of statutes**:
   - The Electoral Act 1993 cross-references the Constitution
     Act 1986 (term of Parliament, House composition) and
     the NZ Bill of Rights Act 1990 (right to vote and
     stand) in tightly-defined places. The s. 268 list
     includes Constitution Act 1986 s. 17 and s. 35
     explicitly, knitting the two statutes together for the
     entrenchment regime.
   - The Local Electoral Act 2001 is a separate statute
     covering local government; cross-reference to the
     Electoral Act 1993 is selective (e.g., voter eligibility
     piggybacks on the parliamentary roll).
   - The Māori Electoral Option provisions (Electoral Act
     1993 ss. 76–77 with Representation Commission
     provisions at s. 28 and population formula at s. 45)
     are tightly interconnected within the Act itself;
     locating the full Māori-electorate machinery requires
     reading these sections together.

3. **Capacity for minority representation**:
   - **Dedicated minority representation**: the Māori
     electorate seats provide a statutory floor of Māori
     parliamentary representation at the population-driven
     count under s. 45. The seven 2023-Parliament Māori
     electorate MPs are the project's clearest documented
     example of dedicated descriptive seats operating
     successfully within a working PR system.
   - **Diffuse minority representation**: the national list
     tier with national Sainte-Laguë allocation aggregates
     dispersed minority support effectively. Māori,
     Pacific, and Asian MPs have entered Parliament through
     party-list selections at every election since 1996.
   - **Self-organised minority parties**: Te Pāti Māori
     (formerly the Māori Party) is the project's clearest
     documented case of an ethnically-grounded party that
     has entered Parliament repeatedly under MMP — once via
     party-vote threshold (2008 — 2.4% party vote, 5
     electorate seats), and once via electorate-only
     backdoor (2023 — 3.08% party vote, 6 electorate
     seats). The interaction of dedicated electorate seats
     with the threshold backdoor (s. 191(4)(b)) is the
     specific mechanism that has enabled this.

### Stakeholder Satisfaction

Generally high. The Electoral Commission's biennial elector
survey records consistently high confidence in election
administration (≥80% of respondents in 2023 stated they had
confidence in the conduct of the election). Public opinion on
MMP has remained majority-supportive since 1996 — the 2011
confirming referendum recorded 57.8% support for retaining
MMP.

The principal ongoing reform debates concern:

- **Threshold reduction** (Independent Electoral Review 2023
  recommended lowering to 3.5%; status: under government
  consideration);
- **Voting age** (current age of 18; advocacy for 16-year-
  old vote, particularly for local elections);
- **Prisoner voting** (current bar at 3+ year sentences;
  legislative review ongoing);
- **Māori Electoral Option implementation** (continuous
  option introduced 2022; operational data accumulating).

is the most live compliance question.

## 9. Gardner portability assessment for US implementation

| Provision | Type | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Single-paper two-vote MMP ballot (Electoral Act 1993 s. 150) | **Universal** | The two-vote single-paper ballot architecture is portable to any MMP design. The randomised ordering by lot (s. 150(3)) is independently portable. |
| Sainte-Laguë / Webster algorithmic codification (Electoral Act 1993 s. 191, Schedule 1A) | **Universal** | The algorithmic enumeration of divisor sequence and rank-allocate procedure is portable to any list-allocation regime. The Schedule 1A drafting form is unusually clean and lifts directly. |
| 5%-or-one-electorate threshold (Electoral Act 1993 s. 191(4)) | **Universal** | The combination of percentage threshold and electorate-seat backdoor is portable. The substantive design choice (which percentage; which backdoor) is a separate question for the importing jurisdiction. |
| Statutory entrenchment of reserved provisions (Electoral Act 1993 s. 268) | **Universal** | The drafting device — closed list of reserved provisions amendable only by referendum or supermajority — is directly portable. The specific 75% supermajority threshold and the referendum option are tunable parameters. |
| Population-driven Māori electorate count (Electoral Act 1993 s. 45) | **Hybrid** | The substantive principle — number of dedicated seats scales with the represented population's roll choice — is portable. The South Island anchor and Census-driven recomputation are specific drafting devices that may or may not transfer. |
| Self-declared descent eligibility (Electoral Act 1993 s. 77) | **Universal** | Self-declaration as the eligibility test for a dedicated-seat roll is a portable drafting move. It avoids the state-administered quantum test problem documented in some US tribal-enrolment regimes. |
| Continuous Māori Electoral Option (Electoral (Māori Electoral Option) Legislation Act 2022) | **Universal** | Continuous roll-switching availability, subject only to a pre-election freeze, is portable to any dedicated-seat regime. |
| Independent Crown Entity EMB structure (Electoral Act 1993 Part 1; Crown Entities Act 2004) | **Universal** | The single consolidated independent EMB structure is portable, although the specific Independent-Crown-Entity legal form is a New Zealand statutory category. |
| Statutory minimum House size with overhang permitted (Electoral Act 1993 ss. 36, 191(5)) | **Particular** | The choice to permit overhang rather than enforcing a fixed total (contrast: Germany post-2023 BWahlG § 4(2) Zweitstimmendeckung) is a New Zealand design choice. The drafting pattern is portable, but the design choice is jurisdiction-specific. |
| Same-day voter enrolment with special declaration vote (Electoral Act 1993 s. 86) | **Universal** | Same-day enrolment is directly portable. The "special declaration" mechanism that defers the verification step until after polling is a clean drafting move. |
| Closed party list with no independents on list (Electoral Act 1993 s. 127) | **Universal** | The closed-list-with-electorate-independents-only architecture is portable. It mirrors the German federal pattern and contrasts with the Scottish individual-regional-candidate option. |
| Boundary review by Representation Commission with statutory criteria (Electoral Act 1993 s. 28, s. 45) | **Universal** | Independent boundary review with statutory population-equality criteria and no legislative override is directly portable. |
| Treaty-of-Waitangi constitutional context for Māori representation | **Particular** | The constitutional foundation of dedicated Māori representation is specific to New Zealand's bicultural history. The underlying drafting device (population-driven dedicated seats with self-declared eligibility) is portable independent of this constitutional context. |

### Top three takeaways for US state/local implementation

1. **Statutory partial entrenchment of core electoral
   provisions via s. 268.** The Electoral Act 1993 s. 268
   mechanism — a closed list of reserved provisions amendable
   only by referendum majority or 75% of all members of the
   House — is the project's most direct documented model of
   surgical statutory entrenchment. A US state statute that
   designates specified sections (assembly size, district
   structure, allocation formula, dedicated-seat provisions)
   as amendable only by supermajority or referendum
   replicates the effect without requiring constitutional
   amendment. The Scotland Act 1998 s. 31(1A) provision is
   functionally similar; the New Zealand version is older
   (1993 vs. 2016), more surgical (specific sections listed),
   and offers two amendment paths rather than one. For a US
   state with an entrenched-elections-clause in its
   constitution but ordinary-majority-statute control of
   electoral mechanics elsewhere, the New Zealand model is
   the documented working case.

2. **Sainte-Laguë / Webster as algorithmically codified
   procedure.** Schedule 1A of the Electoral Act 1993
   specifies the Webster/Sainte-Laguë procedure
   algorithmically — divisor sequence (1, 3, 5, 7, …),
   quotient computation, rank-and-allocate procedure. The
   form is unusually direct and lifts cleanly into US
   statutory drafting. A US state adopting Webster/Sainte-
   Laguë for legislative seat allocation can model its
   schedule directly on Schedule 1A. The procedural-without-
   eponym drafting pattern is consistent with the broader
   pattern documented across this project's case set
   (Germany BWahlG § 5; Scotland Act 1998 s. 8).

3. **Dedicated descriptive-representation seats with
   population-driven count and self-declared roll choice.**
   The combination of (a) Māori electorate seats determined
   by population formula (s. 45), (b) self-declared descent
   eligibility (s. 77), (c) continuous roll-choice
   availability (s. 76 as amended in 2022), and (d) Boundary
   Commission representation including Māori members (s. 28),
   is the project's most developed documented model of
   dedicated minority representation embedded in a PR
   architecture. For US contexts considering dedicated
   descriptive seats — most relevant for tribal
   representation in state legislatures with significant
   Native American populations, or for Black/Latino
   dedicated seats in non-partisan local contexts — the
   New Zealand model provides the documented working
   architecture. The four design elements are independently
   portable; their integration is the New Zealand
   contribution.
