# Performance framework: PROSeS adapted for legislative design

Source: Toby S. James (2020), *Comparative Electoral Management:
Performance, Networks and Instruments*, ch. 4 — the PROSeS framework.
James designs PROSeS to evaluate Electoral Management Bodies; we adapt
it to evaluate **electoral statutes and the administrative regimes they
create**. The five clusters become design criteria for model
legislation: a well-drafted statute should give institutional cover for
each, and a foreign statute can be diagnosed by where it provides such
cover and where it does not.

The five clusters anchor on **democratic ideals** (Beetham 1994:
political equality + popular control of government). Below are the
clusters and their sub-criteria as set out in James's Table 4.2,
re-presented as legislative-design questions.

---

## 1. Process Design

How does the law shape the *internal decision-making process* of
election administration?

- **Public participation** — Does the statute create channels for
  citizens and groups to shape the design and revision of the electoral
  process? (Standing committees, advisory boards, citizens' assemblies,
  notice-and-comment for regulations.)
- **Probity and impartiality** — Does the statute require proper use
  of public funds and require electoral officials to act without
  partisan advantage? (Civil-service-style protections for EMB staff,
  recusal rules, conflict-of-interest disclosure.)
- **Accountability** — Does the statute provide redress when something
  goes wrong, with deadlines, accessible forums, and disclosure of
  performance data?

## 2. Resource Investment

What does the law say about the funding and sustainability of the
electoral system?

- **Sufficiency** — Statutory floor on EMB budgets, or a mechanism that
  ties funding to demonstrated need.
- **Transparency** — Mandatory open accounting of EMB income and
  expenditure.
- **Sustainability** — Multi-cycle funding stability (independence
  from annual political budget fights).
- **Legitimacy** — Restrictions on funding sources that could
  compromise perceived independence (e.g., foreign funds, sectoral
  donors).
- **Contingency** — Statutory authority and reserves for unexpected
  events (cyber incidents, natural disasters, equipment failure).

James (Table 4.1) distinguishes five **investment types** that any
statute must implicitly cover:

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| Sustained | Ongoing infrastructure (registration, EMB premises) |
| Event-related | Costs of a specific contest |
| Security | Physical and cyber security around the contest |
| Campaign | Public funding for candidates/parties (postage, airtime) |
| Project | Multi-cycle reform implementation (e.g. new register) |

## 3. Service Output Quality

What does the law require the administration to *produce*?

- **Convenience** — Ease of registering and voting (hours, locations,
  documentation, online tools, advance/postal voting, day-of
  registration).
- **Accuracy** — Statutory standards for ballot printing, count
  procedures, technology certification, and audit trails.
- **Enforcement** — Whether statutory rules are *binding* and
  uniformly applied, with consequences for non-compliance.
- **Efficiency** — Whether the law authorizes, and the EMB achieves,
  the *optimal* (not maximal) cost per output.

## 4. Service Outcomes

What outcomes does the law aim to produce, and what does it permit us
to measure?

- **Formal effectiveness** — Voter turnout; register accuracy and
  completeness; cases of electoral fraud; rejected ballots; service
  denial; violence.
- **Equity** — Distribution of registration and turnout by gender,
  age, ethnicity, disability, geography, income.
- **Diffuse impact** — Civic engagement, integration with other
  government services, side-effects (positive or negative).
- **Cost per unit of outcome** — Cost per registration and per vote
  cast (distinguished from cost per *output*).

## 5. Stakeholder Satisfaction

Whose voice does the law require the administration to hear?

- **Citizen satisfaction** — Mandates for post-election surveys,
  complaints handling, public reporting.
- **Staff satisfaction** — Working conditions of poll workers and EMB
  staff, often invisible in legislation but consequential.
- **Parties / civil society** — Confidence of political actors and
  pressure groups in the integrity of the process; statutory rights to
  observe, audit, and contest.

## Methodological notes from James

- PROSeS is **case-based and small-n** — it explicitly resists data-
  reduction into a single quality score because comprehensiveness and
  context are more useful for practitioners than parsimony.
- **Absence of evidence is itself part of the assessment.** If a
  jurisdiction does not publish data on a PROSeS dimension, that opacity
  is a finding about the regime, not just a gap in our research.
- The framework is **critical-realist**: aimed at improving practice,
  not just describing it. We adopt the same posture — every case study
  ends with a "what is portable" assessment.

---

## How PROSeS slots into this project

We are not running PROSeS audits of EMBs. We are using the five
clusters as a **checklist of legislative-design objectives**:

1. When reading a foreign statute, ask: which provisions support each
   of the five PROSeS dimensions, and how well?
2. When the foreign statute is silent, ask: does that silence reflect
   reliance on general administrative law, on constitutional doctrine,
   on professional norms — or is it an actual gap?
3. When drafting model US legislation, ensure each provision can be
   traced to a PROSeS rationale.

The PROSeS axis is therefore *evaluative* — it lets us ask, of each
structural choice, "what is this provision trying to accomplish, and
how would we know if it succeeded?"
