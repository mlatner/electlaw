# Component 1 — Seat Product (Assembly Size × District Magnitude)

The first of the three primary components from the Guinier Project
primer (*Getting from Votes to Seats*, pp. 3–8). The Seat Product is
the mathematical product of two distinct statutory choices that,
taken together, set the structural ceiling on how diverse a coalition
can be while still winning representation.

- **Assembly Size (S)** — total number of seats elected to the body.
- **District Magnitude (M)** — number of seats elected per district.
  Political scientists treat M as **the decisive factor** in
  determining how permissive an electoral system is.

> **Seat Product = S × M**

## Threshold of Exclusion

The mathematical lower bound on the share of votes a group must win
to guarantee a seat in a district:

> **Threshold of Exclusion = 1 / (M + 1)**

A 5-seat district has a threshold of 1/6 ≈ 17%. A 10-seat district
has a threshold of 1/11 ≈ 9%. A 1-seat district has a threshold of
1/2 = 50%. The threshold of exclusion shapes the strategic behavior
of groups seeking representation: it tells coalitions how concentrated
they need to be to win.

The threshold of exclusion is a structural property of M alone. The
*operative* threshold in any contest depends additionally on the
allocation formula (Component 3) and any statutory minimum threshold.

## Why this is the foundational component

Every other design choice operates on top of the seat product. A
permissive ballot structure (Component 2) and a minority-friendly
allocation formula (Component 3) cannot rescue a system whose seat
product is too low to clear a coalition's natural support. Conversely,
a generous seat product creates room for the other components to
matter.

The primer's central US argument is that many US "at-large" districts
already have substantial M (e.g., 5–10 council seats), but combine
that magnitude with the wrong ballot structure (block plurality vote)
and so deliver winner-take-all outcomes despite the high magnitude.
Switching the ballot/allocation while preserving the magnitude is
often a path to proportional outcomes that requires no change to
existing district geometry.

## Statutory questions for case studies

### Assembly Size

- Where is total assembly size codified — constitution, statute,
  charter, regulation?
- Who has authority to alter it, and at what threshold?
- What is the historical trajectory? (US history shows reductions
  used to dilute minority representation; expansions used as VRA
  remedies — see primer p. 5.)
- Is it set as a fixed count or as a function of population?

### District Magnitude

- Is M uniform across districts, or does it vary?
- Where are district boundaries drawn, and by whom?
- What is the redistricting cadence?
- Are statutory criteria spelled out (population equality, community
  of interest, compactness, partisan fairness, minority protection)?
- Are minority-protective districting criteria enumerated?

### Tiered systems

- Does the case use a tiered system (district + at-large, or
  national-level compensatory tier)?
- What are the magnitudes at each tier?
- Is the upper tier *compensatory* (leveling — produces overall
  proportionality, as in MMP) or *parallel* (added to district
  results without leveling)?

## Connections to other components

- **Component 3 (Allocation)**: at low M, formula choice changes
  outcomes substantially (primer p. 16: Jefferson vs. Webster
  divergence at 3-seat district). At high M, formulas converge.
- **Component 2 (Ballot)**: some ballot structures only function
  well at certain magnitudes (e.g., STV at small M; closed list at
  any M; cumulative vote works at modest M).
- **Connected — districting law**: VRA Section 2, *Reynolds v. Sims*
  one-person-one-vote, state constitutional restrictions on
  legislative size all bear here.

## US-specific considerations

### Constitutional anchors and amendment levers

Many state constitutions and city charters set assembly size and the
basic shape of districts. **Charter amendment** is the typical lever
for local reform; in many cities it requires a ballot-measure
process. State constitutional provisions on legislative size are a
parallel constraint at that level.

### Voting Rights Act Section 2

Both assembly expansion and changes to district magnitude have been
the subject of VRA litigation. The primer's p. 5 table documents key
cases:

- *Williams v. City of Dallas* (1991): 11→15
- *Board of Estimate v. Morris* (1989, NYC): 35→51
- *City of Mobile v. Bolden* (1985, post-VRA amendment): 3→7
- *Dillard v. Crenshaw County* (1980s, multi-AL): various 3→5, 5→7
- *Houston, TX* (1979): 8→14 (Section 5 annexation settlement)
- *Pritchett v. City of Tuskegee* (1972): 3→5

These precedents show that assembly expansion is a familiar VRA
remedy. They also show that pre-existing US local jurisprudence
already supports altering Component 1 for representational ends.

### One-person-one-vote

District magnitudes must be drawn so that per-seat population is
roughly equal *across districts of equal magnitude*. Tiered systems
and varying-magnitude designs (Switzerland, primer p. 7) complicate
this analysis but do not foreclose it. *Reynolds v. Sims* remains
the constitutional baseline.

### The "at-large pitfall"

US "at-large" elections are typically run under block plurality
voting (Component 2), which produces winner-take-all outcomes
despite high magnitude. The primer (pp. 5–8) treats this as a
diagnostic failure, not a property of high-M designs. Reform
proposals that preserve magnitude but switch ballot/allocation are
the central US strategy.

### Worked US example: Los Angeles

The primer (p. 8) illustrates a 37-seat LA City Council with nine
3-seat districts plus a 10-seat citywide proportional district. The
9% threshold in the citywide tier would secure representation for
LA's 9% Black population, currently underrepresented.
