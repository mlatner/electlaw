# Component 2 — Ballot Structure

The second of the three primary components from the Guinier Project
primer (*Getting from Votes to Seats*, pp. 9–13). Ballot structure is
the **interface between voter and electoral process**: it determines
how voters express their preferences and, consequently, how groups
can coordinate to win representation.

## The two coordination problems

The primer identifies two coordination problems any ballot must
address (p. 10):

- **Level A — Candidate-side coordination**: managing the *number*
  of candidates and lists that appear. Too few candidates loses
  potential seats; too many spreads support too thin to clear
  thresholds.
- **Level B — Voter-side coordination**: managing the *distribution*
  of votes among candidates. Concentrating too much support on one
  popular candidate wastes votes; spreading too thin loses seats.

Different ballot structures impose different burdens on each level
of coordination. A central design question is whether the law
should solve coordination problems *for* groups (closed-list,
strongly coalition-centered) or leave them to be solved by
candidates and voters in real time (open list and below).

## The ballot-structure spectrum

The primer (pp. 11–12) arrays ballot types on a spectrum from
**candidate-centered** to **coalition-centered**:

| Structure | Coordination burden | Coalition-centered? | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| First-past-the-post / multi-round | Heavy on both levels | No (winner-take-all) | US single-seat districts |
| Limited vote | Heavy on Level A | Partial | PA, NY, AL local (VRA remedy) |
| Cumulative vote | Heavy on Level B | Partial | TX, AL local (VRA remedy) |
| At-large plurality / block vote | Heavy on both | No (most restrictive) | US at-large councils |
| SNTV | Heavy on Level A | Partial | (former) Japan |
| STV | Voter-side intensive | Partial | Scotland local, NSW Senate |
| **Open list** | Moderate on both | **Yes — list level** | DE, FI, NL, BR |
| **Flexible list** | Lighter | **Yes** | BE, SE |
| **Closed list** | Solved by the list itself | **Yes — most coalition-centered** | ES, ZA, IL |
| Above-the-line group voting | List-shaped UI on STV | Yes (as a hybrid) | NSW Senate, NSW local |

For our project, the **list family** (closed / flexible / open) plus
its **non-partisan variants** is the analytic center. The primer
explicitly notes (p. 12):

> "List systems are just as easy to use in non-partisan elections as
> in partisan elections, and a proportional representation system may
> have partisan and non-partisan lists in the same election."

## Non-partisan list labeling — a subsection-level design problem

The guide treats partisan list ballots as the primary case (most
US PR reform at the federal and state level will operate on
partisan ballots). But because most US local charters run formally
non-partisan elections, **non-partisan list labeling is a distinct
subsection** that the guide must address. When formal party labels
are not available, what can a list use as its label? The primer's
options (p. 12):

- A **professional association** (e.g., teachers' association,
  small-business coalition).
- An **advocacy cause** (e.g., parks coalition, transit reform).
- A **geographic region** (e.g., Eastside Neighbors).
- An **ethnic affiliation** (with appropriate guardrails).
- **Generic placeholders**: *Group A, B, C…*

This is the area of US implementation where comparative cases offer
the most direct lessons that have no analog in existing US ballot
law. Working systems abroad have answered this question:

- Germany (*Wählergemeinschaft*, *Wählervereinigung*).
- Spain (*agrupación de electores* under LOREG).
- Netherlands (*lokale partij*).
- Finland (*valitsijayhdistys*, voter association).
- Chile (*candidatura independiente* / pacto independiente).

Statutory rules on what labels are permissible, what verification is
required, and what guardrails exist against deceptive labeling
(party names, government insignia, false geographic claims) are a
non-trivial drafting problem.

## The simplicity premium

The primer argues forcefully (p. 13, citing Taagepera & Shugart) that
complexity is itself an equity problem:

> "the more complex a system becomes, the fewer people can comprehend
> it so as to make use of its opportunities."

Closed-list ballot structures are the most error-resistant:
voters mark a list, and that single mark is unambiguous. Open and
flexible list ballots increase voter choice but also raise rates of
ballot rejection; ranked structures (STV) raise them further. The
primer cites the **NSW Australia above-the-line option** (p. 13) as a
working example: voters can mark a single list square or rank
individual candidates. The vast majority choose the list option.

## Statutory questions for case studies

- What ballot type does the law specify, and in which legal
  instrument (statute, regulation, EMB rule)?
- For list ballots: closed, open, or flexible? With how many
  preference votes per voter? Cumulation or panachage allowed?
- Where is the ballot **physically and legally** designed (text,
  layout, language, ordering of lists, ordering of candidates within
  lists)?
- Treatment of overvotes, undervotes, and stray marks.
- For non-partisan lists specifically:
  - What label / identifier may a list display on the ballot?
  - Are restrictions imposed (no party names, no misleading
    geographic claims, etc.)?
  - Who validates the label?
- Are write-in candidates accommodated, and if so how do they
  interact with the list system?
- Is there an above-the-line option (single-mark list vote in lieu
  of ranking individual candidates)?

## Connections

- **Component 1 (Seat Product)**: ballot complexity tolerance scales
  inversely with district magnitude — voters can rank 5 candidates
  comfortably, 50 less so. Closed list scales unlimitedly.
- **Component 3 (Allocation)**: open-list ballots couple to intra-
  list seat assignment (preference votes reorder); closed-list
  ballots do not.
- **Connected — list / candidate registration**: which entities can
  put a list on the ballot is upstream of ballot structure.
- **Connected — campaign finance**: open lists create candidate-level
  spending dynamics that closed lists do not.

## US-specific considerations

### Existing US ballot law

- *Burdick v. Takushi* (1992) — sets the deferential standard for
  state ballot regulation.
- *Williams v. Rhodes* (1968) and progeny — ballot access for
  non-major-party candidates and groups.
- State ballot-uniformity statutes — many states require a single
  statewide ballot design, complicating jurisdiction-specific PR
  pilots.

### US precedents already on the spectrum

- **Cumulative voting** is in use as a VRA Section 2 remedy in
  selected Texas and Alabama local jurisdictions; statutory
  templates and judicial precedent exist.
- **Limited voting** is in use in some PA, NY, AL local
  jurisdictions; even less constitutionally controversial.
- These precedents establish that *non-plurality* ballot structures
  are constitutionally tractable in US local elections.

### Non-partisan local elections

California, Nebraska's state legislature, and many cities (LA, NYC
for some offices) run formally non-partisan elections. **List
labeling rules for non-party groups are largely undeveloped in US
law** and are an important drafting problem for the local-
applications subsection of the guide.

### Anti-fusion and ballot-uniqueness

Many states forbid a single candidate from appearing on multiple
party lines (anti-fusion). List PR systems generally do not require
fusion, so anti-fusion is not a barrier; but if a model proposes to
allow lists to cross-endorse candidates, anti-fusion rules would
need amendment.

### Write-in handling

US states generally permit write-ins. Under list PR, write-ins must
either be allocated as a separate residual or excluded from the
list-allocation pool. This is a fine-grained drafting issue but a
real one.

## Cross-cutting research questions for the project

When all twelve cases are documented:

1. Which non-party-label categories do working systems actually use,
   and how are deceptive labels prevented?
2. What ballot-error rates are documented for each ballot type, and
   how do they distribute across socioeconomic and racial groups?
3. Where do open-list systems set the preference-vote threshold for
   reordering, and what is the comparative incidence of reordering?
